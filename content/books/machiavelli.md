I had never read Machiavelli before prepping for my [second episode with Ada Palmer](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/ada-palmer-2), which was all about him. (The [first one](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/ada-palmer) was about her book *Inventing the Renaissance*.) These are my notes on *The Prince* and the *Discourses on Livy*: what I found interesting, what I'm still chewing on, and what I couldn't resolve.


## The means matter, a lot

One misconception I had, because I had not read these books before, is that Machiavelli says the means don't matter, only the end. There's a virtue ethics sense in which maybe he doesn't think the means matter. But he is way more concerned about the means than I would've naively thought.

He, more so than anybody before, is interested in the means, because the means by which you achieve power determine how stable and how fruitful that power will be. If you rise up with the help of great powers, or through the favors of men you have wronged in the past, or through hired hands, your power will be ephemeral. He's interested in the means from a consequentialist rather than a deontological perspective.

It's also worth noticing who the advice is for: not a republic or a hereditary monarchy, but a principality won by fortune or arms, which is harder to hold. That makes sense for a book dedicated to the Medici, who are back in power due to an extraordinary piece of fortune: the Pope being a Medici.

## Don't make your rivals stronger (Machiavelli as Kissinger)

One of his key lessons is to not gain power at the cost of making someone of similar power also more powerful. It's much better to ally with weak powers to take down strong ones than the other way around. His examples of the mistake:

- Charles teaming up with Alexander and Cesare Borgia to conquer the Romagna, giving leverage to the papacy.
- Charles inviting Spain, another big powerful nation, into Italy to invade Naples together.
- Inviting the French into Italy in the first place.

## The Borgia puzzle

I still don't fully understand why Machiavelli thinks Cesare Borgia played his cards right.

Take the killing of Remirro de Orco and the Senigallia massacre (same week, December 1502). With the first, Borgia shows that if you as a lieutenant follow his instructions and bring peace using "maximum severity", he will just pin the downsides on you. With the second, he shows: never surrender halfway through a coup, because the bridges are burned. Machiavelli thinks this is brilliant. But aren't these actually bad moves, since they suggest to any rational person that they shouldn't follow you?

Relatedly, should we take the fact that he makes a special point of these betrayals as evidence that they were actually rare? To the extent that everyone was Christian, surely they believed they'd go to hell, or at least spend centuries in purgatory, for breaking their oaths. That he has to argue it's okay not to keep faith suggests oath-breaking wasn't common, which seems very different from the modern day.

## Nobody asks how you got there

Once you're in power, people won't really judge you for how you got there. His examples are Julius betraying Cesare Borgia, and Borgia betraying his mercenaries. As Marriott translates it:

> let a prince have the credit of conquering and holding his state, the means will always be considered honest, and he will be praised by everybody

The implication is that a tyranny might be easier to slide into than people realize. Once the tyrant has shown they're in control of the situation, people won't question how they got that control. They'll just defer to their power.

## Feared vs. loved

> it is much safer to be feared than loved, when, of the two, either must be dispensed with.

What he's getting at, I think, is that he's very cynical about people's nature, and very realistic about what will actually motivate them. If your power base depends on people's promises and loyalties, then as soon as your rule seems to be tattering, they'll go back on them. If your rule depends on people expecting to be punished for breaking their oath, that's much more stable.

He basically thinks people will act as badly as they are allowed to, whether they're the tyrant, the people, or the nobles. That's also why he thinks the checks and balances of both the populace and the nobles are necessary (see the *Discourses* section).

The caveat is to avoid being hated. And the hatred comes more from how people feel they are treated than from your reputation for honesty. <!-- TODO: you flagged wanting to dig into what makes people hate a prince; no conclusions in the notes. -->

## Reputation

Something I didn't appreciate, but Machiavelli makes a huge point of, is how formidable people consider you to be. It matters both for deterring invasion and for extracting concessions.

He thinks Ferdinand of Aragon's shock-and-awe approach is really clever: you always want people engaged in some cool grand project (Granada, the campaigns against Muslims, the African ventures, Italy, France), kept in awe and splendor. Meanwhile no one respects Emperor Maximilian, because they know he can be deterred and talked out of things.

This was literally Machiavelli's job: when Maximilian tried to extract a bribe from Florence to not invade, Florence sent Machiavelli to check whether this guy was for real.

The same logic runs through his other advice:

- **Advisors.** Take advice from a few people you trust and tell them to be totally honest, but let nobody else even think they can sway you. It needs to look like your will is all that matters.
- **Conspiracies.** If a conspirator approaches somebody unwilling to go along, that person has a lot to gain by going straight to the prince. So conspiracies need a lot of people to hate you, and a lot of people to think you won't or can't react strongly. That's the value of a formidable reputation.

## Liberality is worse than miserliness

He really harps on this: it's much better to piss off the few who expect special privileges (they can be neutralized easily) than the whole people, from whom you'd have to extract to placate the few (there are too many to resist). So liberality is worse than miserliness. It's basically a public choice argument.

I'd put it as: it's easier to satisfy the wider elite than the 0.001%. The wider elite just need you not to take their property and their women. The ultra-elite expect special privileges, and granting those requires taking other people's property. So better to disenfranchise the ultra-elite and keep them from harming you than to gnaw at the wider population.

## Expectations and slippery slopes

A big part of his thinking is about managing expectations. People can accommodate a lot once they've come to expect it, but they get up in a tizzy about the smallest things they don't expect. That's why he thinks hereditary principalities are easy to hold and an average person can manage one: people are used to the ruling family's customs. This also illustrates why slippery slopes are real.

His account of taking over a new state (chapter 3) reads to me like a leveraged buyout. You have to lever up to do the M&A, and to pay down the leverage you have to wring the thing you acquired. That wringing is painful. Hence:

> men ought either to be well treated or crushed, because they can avenge themselves of lighter injuries, of more serious ones they cannot

## Judging the decision, not the outcome

As consequentialist as he is, he doesn't judge rulers by what they achieved. He judges them by what they were likely to achieve given their actions. Pope Julius II achieved a lot and is admirable for many of his actions, like betraying Cesare Borgia after becoming Pope in 1503, or personally marching on Bologna. But Machiavelli still thinks that, on net, he was too bold. All his schemes succeeded, but there was a good chance he'd have lost the war against France in 1511. So we should learn from his example *not* to act the way he did. I don't know if it was common at the time to say that even someone who succeeded shouldn't be emulated if they were likely to fail.

## The papacy, and the presidency

In the chapter on ecclesiastical principalities, he explains what kept papal power in check before Alexander Borgia:

- Italy was balanced between five powers: the Pope, Venice, Naples, Milan, and Florence.
- The Orsini and Colonna factions kept each other in check.
- Popes reigned only about 10 years on average, not long enough to crush both factions.
- Each new pope dismantled what the previous one had built.

Does this not describe the presidency today? And after Borgia, why could no pope combine the warlikeness of Julius II with Cesare's shrewdness and unify Italy?

## The Discourses

There's a common misconception that Machiavelli supported monarchy. In the *Discourses* he argues that Rome achieved its greatness when it was free, when it could turn the friction between the nobles and the people to its advantage. His clear-sightedness about human nature serves liberty, at least here. He's right that people will do whatever they feel they can get away with.

### Liberty needs a founder

Book I, chapters 9 and 10: creating liberty depends on one man first consolidating power, because many people can't agree on the original design before institutions exist. Romulus doesn't hand power to an heir. He hands it to a system of government and establishes a Senate. <!-- TODO: your note says "Romulus kicking out the Tarquins"; the Tarquins were expelled later, by Brutus. Phrase dropped; check what you meant. -->

1787 was a Machiavellian founding moment: a republic saved by violating its own constitutional order. The Articles required every state legislature to ratify amendments; the framers changed that to nine states to get the Constitution passed.

### Religion

I find what he says about religion extremely interesting. In chapter 11, a founder isn't enough. He also needs religion to give the institutions authority and make them last beyond the lifetime of a wise prince. Numa, who made religion the foundation of Rome, matters more than Romulus.

His example is Scipio after Cannae. Hannibal has destroyed the Roman armies and Romans are about to abandon their country. Scipio goes down with his sword and makes them swear by their gods to stay. The oath alone is enough: Hannibal can't be worse than the gods. <!-- TODO: your note says they were fleeing "for Sicily"; check against the text. -->

And he doesn't like the Church of his day. He thinks it's too corrupt to give people morals or give legitimacy to an honorable regime. It has also kept Italy divided: too weak to unite Italy itself, but strong enough to stop anyone else from doing it, and when threatened it calls in foreigners.

### People used to tyranny

Chapter 16: people used to tyranny find it hard to sustain a free government. The nobles who benefited from the tyranny are bitter against the new regime, while those freed from oppression are no longer grateful just for not being robbed or harmed.

Isn't this exactly what happened in Russia after the czars, and again after the Communist Party collapsed?

### Checks and balances

His insight about Rome, that you need friction between the democratic, aristocratic, and monarchical elements of government, seems perfectly embodied in our three branches. His justification is not unlike the American founders' reason for pitting factions against each other: people will act as badly as you allow them to.

Ada pointed out that the separation of powers in the US Constitution actually came via Montesquieu's *Spirit of the Laws*, with a line running from Machiavelli through Hobbes and Locke to Montesquieu.

### What we've solved, and what we haven't

In chapter 20 he says it's incredibly rare to get two great princes in a row in a hereditary monarchy (Philip and then Alexander of Macedon), whereas a republic could in theory have a thousand great princes in a row, since it can choose the most meritorious person in the whole population. Realistically, even democracies rarely solve the political economy problems and create good incentives for rulers, much less elect the most able people every cycle. Ada's response was to point to his argument that all institutions are gradually corrupted and need periodic renewal, or they slowly become dysfunctional.

That leaves me with a question. A science manual from 500 years ago would be a historical curiosity. But you can read the *Discourses*, about a system 2,000 years older than Machiavelli, and find the same themes as today. Why is there so much less progress in political institutions? Many technical systems get more robust over time. If a piece of open source software has existed for 30 years, it's harder to find bugs in it now than it was 30 years ago. Why is it so much harder to consolidate patches in political institutions?

Some things we have solved that he's obsessed with:

- **Justice.** A lot of *The Prince* and the *Discourses* is about making sure a family isn't enraged that their son was killed and it went unavenged. A reliable criminal justice system makes that problem go away. And I feel like we don't hear about conspiracies much anymore.
- **Loyalty to the regime.** One reason Rome fell, he says, is that generals spent years on far-off frontiers (Caesar, for one) commanding men who were loyal to them, not to Rome.

Fast communication, impartial justice, and an impartial welfare state that cuts out the patrons seem to be what a regime needs for enough legitimacy, and so stability, to become a modern nation state.

## A book for one reader

*The Prince* wasn't unusual for being written for a single reader. As Ada explained, elites often paid scholars to write a handbook just for them or their kids. Some survived, like *The Prince*. Maybe most are lost.

The funny thing is that maybe the era of books written to be published widely was the exception, and we're returning to the Renaissance standard. Already at least half the words I read on a given day are generated specifically for me by AI. AI can't yet write a literary masterpiece that everybody would want to read. But eventually it will, and we might be back to masterpieces read by only one or two people.
