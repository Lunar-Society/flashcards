For the last year and a half, I’ve been skeptical of the idea that we’re close to AGI. [Last June, I said](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/timelines-june-2025):

> Eventually the models will be able to learn on the job in the subtle organic way that humans can. However, it’s just hard for me to see how that could happen within the next few years, given that there’s no obvious way to slot in online, continual learning into the kinds of models these LLMs are.

I’ve also been skeptical of the idea that we’re close to automating AI research. Here is [me last December](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/thoughts-on-ai-progress-dec-2025):

> Somehow this automated researcher is going to figure out the algorithm for AGI—a problem humans have been banging their head against for the better part of a century—while not having the basic learning capabilities that children have? I find this super implausible.

But I’ve changed my mind. After spending the last 3 months thinking about these topics and debating  people, both on and off my podcast, I now think that we’re only a couple of years away from AIs that are better than humans at AI research. I’ve also come to believe that automating AI research will compress so much progress into a single year that, by 2030, we’ll have AIs that are at least as good as the best human in every field—as good as Steve Jobs at building companies, Lyndon Johnson at wheeling and dealing, and Einstein at making new scientific breakthroughs.

## There are no AI workers yet, but there will be soon

But before we get to the AIs of 2030, let’s talk about current AIs. Today’s models are capable of doing well-defined, computer-based tasks (sometimes superhumanly well), but they can’t reliably own projects that are ambiguously scoped and long-running. For example, you can’t have an AI run a business for you, and AIs have [not yet fully automated almost any white-collar jobs](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/tracking-impact-ai-labor-market).

In most domains, the AIs of today are like highly educated but derpy and amnesiac interns. If you’ve ever worked with interns, you know they can be very hit or miss. But the AIs of 2028 will be more like an industry veteran you poached from another company—they’ll still need to do a bit of onboarding, but they’ll be experienced professionals who know how to ramp up fast at a new organization. Also, they’ll keep improving over time, instead of getting Men-in-Black mind-zapped at the end of the day.

So when you think about the AIs of 2028, you shouldn’t be thinking about current chatbot LLMs. You should think, instead, of a smart, competent, always-cranking human colleague, who happens to be living on a server. Here are the reasons why.

### Context lengths will expand

I’ve spent the past year and half [arguing](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/timelines-june-2025) that the main thing missing from today’s AIs is the ability to improve from on-the-job experience.  Every new session is their first day on the job. And in each session, they can only retain a couple of hours’ worth of information, so they don’t accumulate the feedback or know-how that arrives over the course of weeks or months.

I still think we need to solve this problem before we get useful AI workers. But I’ve changed my mind about how difficult solving it will be. Previously, I thought we needed some crazy breakthrough to allow models to update their own weights at the end of every session. But current AIs are already capable of learning within a session, without any weight updates. And I now think we're on track to have sessions that can be many months long (through one of [the](https://jessylin.com/2025/10/20/continual-learning/) [many](https://labs.baseten.co/articles/towards-infinite-context-windows-neural-kv-cache-compaction) [promising](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06266) [techniques](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06266) that AI labs are exploring to dramatically increase effective context lengths). So while the problem of updating the weights may remain very difficult, I don’t think it needs to be solved in order to unlock a single worker’s ability to learn on the job.[^1]

### RL training will generalize

Another key problem is that AIs currently lack the street-smarts necessary even to get started in unfamiliar situations. The way we’re currently making AIs smarter is by having them do lots of tasks that are easy to automatically grade from within a data center: Do the million new lines of code the AI wrote pass the unit tests? Did the AI solve more open math problems? But of course, that’s not what most jobs are actually like. I talked about this in a [recent essay](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/the-next-paradigm):

> How would we train an AI to build a business? How would you make an AI that’s really good at winning court cases? Or having a profitable day trading in the markets? Or helping a candidate win an election? The rollout requires interacting with the world and cannot be recreated simply within the datacenter.

These kinds of things are hard to grade programmatically, which makes them difficult to directly train on. But I’ve come to think that AIs that are well trained on far more high quality verifiable tasks will still be very capable in these real-world domains.

That’s because getting really good at any complex task takes two things. One is domain-specific knowledge. The other is a more general set of skills—the kind that capable humans have—like being able to seek out relevant information, make a plan, push forward on it through mistakes and obstacles, keep track of what you’ve learned, and collaborate with other people.

Let me be clear: I’m not saying we can bake the answer to every real-world problem into the weights. [Reality has a surprising amount of detail](https://johnsalvatier.org/blog/2017/reality-has-a-surprising-amount-of-detail), and you can’t prepare for all of it in advance. My claim is that with enough training on verifiable tasks, individual AI instances will have the skills necessary to ramp up in an unfamiliar context, so that over the course of a few months of learning on a new job, they’ll be as valuable as the 95th-percentile human working in that field.

We already see clear examples of this kind of generalization: if you point Claude Code or Codex at a project it has never seen before, it knows how to orient itself, figure out how everything fits together, and then tackle an ambitious assignment. And even though these general skills are currently mostly limited to coding, it’s important to keep in mind that we’re still pretty early in this RL paradigm.

Within another two years, we’ll probably have over 100 times more RL environments, many of them built by AIs themselves, and they’ll be far more diverse, realistic, and sprawling than today’s. So as long as the skills that help in tasks we can check overlap even moderately with the skills needed for tasks we can’t, AIs should get much better at the messy, real-world work that makes up most jobs.

One important piece of evidence for this transfer hypothesis is that we’re already seeing models’ intelligence generalize. Of course, it’s hard to measure how much models have improved at tasks that are not easily verifiable, because if we could… those tasks would be verifiable. But you can go talk to a new model and compare it to an old one. In my experience, AIs have gotten much better at things that have very little to do with code or math—for example, helping me research the abstract questions I needed to answer to write this essay.

#### Human evolution shows that intelligence is general

The other big piece of evidence is human evolution. Our general-purpose mental architecture was developed by evolution, which is an optimization process with a really crude verifier: Did you pass your genes on or not? In the ancestral environment, the tasks which influenced that outcome were things like hunting, fending off predators, and keeping your standing in a tribe.

These are a tiny subset of the kinds of tasks humans are able to tackle today. Evolution did not specifically optimize us to be able to write a Pulitzer Prize-winning biography, or make advanced semiconductors, or lead nations of millions of people, or come up with a new branch of mathematics. But, in optimizing us for survival on the savanna, evolution built a general-purpose learner.

In training these AIs, we’re running a similar process, except we’re doing so deliberately, with tasks more closely related to those needed for success in today’s world. And unlike evolution, AI companies are *trying* to improve transfer during training (for example, by making sure that performance improves on difficult tasks that were held out of training). If dumb, blind evolution can get this much transfer out of the savanna (in fact, *has to* in order to build a creature effective at passing on its genes), the deliberate effort that AI companies are putting into building a flexible agent should advance much further.

### AIs of 2028 will still be jagged

What will it be like to use the AIs of 2028?

Suppose the CEO of a Fortune 500 company wanted to make a 2028 AI into his chief of staff. How quickly could it ramp up? I think much faster than most people anticipate. On its first morning, it could read every email, Slack message and design doc the company has ever produced. By the end of the first day, copies of it could have interviewed every employee and major client in parallel. Even a single copy of the AI is gaining experience much faster, because it’s working 10 times faster than any human and doing so around the clock. All of this together means that within a few months on the job, this AI would have built up a magnitude of experience that would take a human over a year or more to accumulate.

Another important thing to keep in mind about the AI of 2028 is that using it will be less like hiring a single human employee and more like recruiting an entire department: [AIs are so much cheaper than humans](https://defensesindepth.bio/when-they-can-perform-a-task-ais-are-much-cheaper-than-humans/) that companies will be able to spin up automated mini-firms to own full initiatives. And while AIs are currently worse than humans at collaborating, I expect them to get really good at working together in teams. Part of why is that AI companies will be specifically training this capability. But also AIs have inherent advantages for coordination: They can share their entire context with new copies of themselves, and they can read each other’s work at superhuman speed.​​

To be clear, I’m not saying that two more years of RL training and overall AI progress will get you the totality of human intelligence. I expect even the AIs of 2028 will be spiky, just as today’s AIs are spiky: better than the best humans at some things, worse than them at others. But they'll be much more useful than today's AIs. At jobs that don't require physical labor, the AIs of 2028 will be more useful to your firm than all but the top 5% of your employees.

But I don’t think jaggedly human-level AI is where this story ends, or really where we’ll spend much actual time. That’s because, by the time we have AIs that can do most jobs passably well, we’ll already have AIs that are as good as the best humans at one job in particular: building better AIs. At that point, the speed and character of AI progress will hinge on the dynamics of recursive self-improvement.

## AIs are already helping with AI research

Before we get into the theoretical arguments about what happens once we automate AI development, it’s worth noting that the leading AI labs have reported that current AIs are already making AI researchers more productive.

OpenAI published a [blog post](https://openai.com/index/research-acceleration-view-inside-openai/) about this in September, where they showed that the heaviest 10% of AI users among OpenAI’s researchers went from using around $1,000 a day of internal coding agents at the beginning of the summer to over $7,000 a day by the end of August.

![](/series/how-i-see-the-situation/fig-2.png)

In September, Anthropic put out a similar [post](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/measuring-pace-of-ai-development) on its own AI uplift. They reported that Claude now “leads” 26% of Anthropic’s AI R&D work—up from under 1% as recently as February.[^2]

If you made me guess, I think AI is increasing the speed of AI progress at the frontier by somewhere between 25% to 50% (keep in mind that the labs are using more powerful models internally than the ones you and I have access to[^3]).

## Math is a preview of what will happen to AI research

When I think about how big a deal it could be if AIs automate AI research, and how soon that could happen, a really important update for me has been just seeing how quickly AI capabilities in mathematics have exploded. Current AIs are slicing through well-scoped problems in mathematics like a hot knife through butter. It was only two years ago that [I asked Dario](https://youtu.be/Nlkk3glap_U?t=2135) why LLMs aren’t able to use their vast knowledge across so many different fields in order to connect different ideas and make new discoveries. And lo and behold, here we are: In a wholly verifiable domain like mathematics, AIs are doing this search-and-match discovery to such a great extent that [hundreds of important results](https://openai.com/index/sharing-ai-progress-in-mathematics/) (which the top mathematicians and computer scientists in the world had spent decades trying to resolve) are being churned out as side quests to [hype future model releases](https://openai.com/index/ten-advances-in-mathematics/) or [pass the time during World Cup matches](https://x.com/__alpoge__/status/2079028340955197566).

It’s crazy how fast we went from, in 2023, “This model can’t reliably add four-digit numbers,” to, in 2024, “This model can barely solve any problems in a high school math competition,” to, in 2025, “Oh, it can win a Gold Medal at the International Math Olympiad,” to, earlier this year, “Wow, it’s solving open Erdős problems, but maybe just because no human has attempted to solve these particular problems, and a similar solution was already in the literature,” to, about a month ago, “Okay, it fucking solved a Millennium Prize problem in 88 hours.”

There’s a line in the novel *East of Eden*: “And there is one sure thing about the fall of gods: they do not fall a little; they crash and shatter.”

It’s true that progress has skewed disproportionately towards the verification-laden parts of math, like finding counterexamples (rather than coming up with new theories, say). Toby Ord posted a great essay about this recently. He [writes](https://www.tobyord.com/writing/mathematics-is-more-than-proof), “While current AI systems are starting to automate mathematical proof, there is currently very little evidence that they can ask powerful questions or find profound new fields of mathematical enquiry.”

But another way to say the same point would be: *Current AI systems have solved a Millennium Prize problem, all without the ability to ask powerful questions or find profound new fields of mathematical inquiry.*

And this ability to solve well-posed (but still incredibly difficult) problems is going to be an even bigger deal for machine learning research than for mathematics, broadly constructed. That’s because the goal of mathematics research is not just to prove or disprove long lists of conjectures, but to extend human understanding of mathematical concepts. Coming up with the idea of topology, group theory, or even the Cartesian grid matters far more than knowing exactly [how many one-unit-apart pairs you can make from n dots](https://openai.com/index/model-disproves-discrete-geometry-conjecture/). We mainly care about such abstruse questions insofar as they might elicit new mathematical theory.

But in AI research, the priorities are exactly reversed. AI labs mainly care about how any new algorithmic recipe empirically makes training more efficient or elicits smarter models. They don’t care about theory for its own sake, and all the progress we’ve made in deep learning over the last decade and half has not required much new theory anyways. The equivalent of grinding through random conjectures would be enormously valuable in machine learning in a way that it just isn’t in mathematics.

AI research is actually surprisingly similar to the type of mathematical progress which current AIs are disproportionately good at: unlocking a ton of low hanging fruit by applying a combination of incredible persistence and a superhuman capacity to connect ideas from lots of adjacent domains.

For example, let’s talk about the transformer architecture. My friend [Sholto Douglas](https://x.com/_sholtodouglas), who is an AI researcher, has a perennially half-finished blog post where he explains how you could have invented the transformer two years earlier than people actually did. Maybe this essay will get him to quit stalling and complete it, but in the meantime, I’ll summarize a bit of the discussion I had with him about the contents of that essay.

Before the [Transformer paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762) was published in 2017, many researchers had already identified attention as a way to better parallelize training and communicate information across longer sequences. Chris Olah, who was an active researcher then, has [said](https://x.com/ch402/status/1926753580762984730): “At the time, my sense is that the transformer architecture was a natural combination of many ideas (attention, residual networks) which had independently been effective, taken to quite aggressive lengths.” In other words, none of the core pieces were a surprise to researchers working in the field.

So then why didn’t people invent the transformer sooner? One reason was that this new architecture was only obviously better than the state-of-the-art once you connected half a dozen additional tweaks and innovations. And though all of these had been in the literature for years,[^4] no one knew how to combine the puzzle pieces. Doing so required research taste, of course, but also tons and tons of small experiments.

But what I’ve just described above—persistently and intelligently matching dozens of different combinations of ideas—is exactly the kind of capability that AIs have shown themselves disproportionately skilled at in their contributions to mathematics.

Even the transformer, which is arguably the biggest breakthrough in ML since AlexNet kicked off the deep learning revolution in 2012, is the kind of thing that Claude 7 could invent if Chris Olah asked it to try hard to get this attention to work better.[^5]

I want to clarify that some parts of AI research are, in fact, less verifiable than math. That’s why OpenAI hasn’t fired all of its researchers, despite having a model that can autonomously solve Millenium Prize problems. But I think AI research is verifiable enough that AIs will soon be as good at it as the best humans. Explaining why requires getting into the nitty-gritty of how AI research actually works, and because those details are interesting but technical, I’ve saved them for the Appendix–if you’re curious, just keep reading after the conclusion. But in the meantime, I’ll give you my estimate of when we get there.

If I had to pick my modal year for when automated researchers are as good as the very best human AI researchers–the equivalent of Ilya Sutskever and Alec Ratford and Noam Shazeer–I’d guess 2028.

Why do I think AIs could get this good within two years? In every domain where we can check answers and give AIs thousands of rounds of practice—for example, competitive programming contests, or well-scoped open math problems—they’ve gone from worse than most humans to better than the best human within a few years. As I argued above, AI research has those same verifiable properties.

Of course, a big part of what makes Ilya so good can’t be directly verified—for example, his ability to bet, years in advance, on the right research direction. But as we’ve seen in math, models generalize far beyond what they were directly trained on. OpenAI’s model was obviously not trained by solving other Millennium Prize problems. It was trained on lots of less ambitious, more tractable math problems, and from those it generalized all the way to solving a problem no human had ever cracked. I’d expect the same in AI research, where the combination of thousands of rounds of practice on verifiable research tasks, plus the fact that each new generation of models is broadly smarter than the last, will make models excellent even at the parts of AI research we can’t directly check.

I want to be clear that there’s a pretty decent chance that hitting this milestone takes longer because some important part of AI research remains hard to automate. I’d guess there’s a 25% chance that it doesn’t happen until after 2032.[^6] But I also think things could move much faster, and we hit this milestone in 2027. Some of the most well-informed researchers I know, people who have had a very good track record of prediction over the last few years, expect AI to beat the best humans at AI research as early as next year.

## Automating the best researchers would make algorithmic progress five times faster than today

And what happens then? How much faster would algorithmic progress be at the moment the best AI research are fully automated? There’s three important effects we need to dig through.

### Faster

The first effect is that automated Ilya would be operating much faster. Today’s AIs already do work at about 10 times human speed.[^7] And automated researchers would also be working around the clock, nights and weekends included. As a result, each individual automated researcher will be cramming roughly 50x more work every calendar week than a human researcher can do.

Of course, that doesn’t mean AI progress will immediately accelerate 50x. Those automated researchers would still be bottlenecked by compute for experiments, which aren’t running any faster. So there would be diminishing returns. But diminishing by how much?

My intuition comes from a [great essay by Eli Lifland and Ryan Greenblatt](https://blog.aifutures.org/p/slow-corporations-as-an-intuition). They lay out an interesting thought experiment: imagine a “slow” version of OpenAI (let’s call it SlothAI). It has the same compute as the real OpenAI, but its researchers only work 1 hour every week. They’re just the laziest researchers in the world. How much slower would SlothAI make progress? It’s definitely not 50 times slower—experiments can keep running even when the researchers doze off for 98% of the week. But how many careful experiments can the SlothAI folks really design and analyze with just one hour of labor? SlothAI would be forced to be far more wasteful with its compute than the real OpenAI. It seems to me that, intuitively, SlothAI would end up making algorithmic progress at least five times slower.

But compared to a company full of automated researchers, OpenAI *is* SlothAI. So whatever makes SlothAI less productive (for example, the fact that their researchers get much less time to brainstorm different ideas and analyze and design experiments) also applies to OpenAI. Human thinking speed is a biological accident. It would be a strange coincidence if our speed happened to be exactly the point beyond which thinking faster was no longer helpful.

As a result, my guess is that you get a five times speedup in algorithmic progress from the fact that the automated researchers will be doing 50 times the amount of work in a single calendar week.

### Smarter

Which brings us to the second effect, namely that this is before we account for the fact that every automated researcher will be at least as good at AI research and engineering as the very best humans.

My sense is that around 80% of the algorithmic progress at a given frontier lab is driven by its top 50 researchers. It’s not for nothing that Zuckerberg was willing to pay hundreds of millions of dollars to acquire the best talent in the industry.

That said, the thousandth Ilya-level researcher is worth much less than the 10th because AI development doesn’t just float on big new breakthroughs—it also requires lots of boring but careful schlepwork. Having a world class genius filter your data doesn’t speed you up that much.

Putting all this together, my guess is that having every single engineer and researcher be at the level of the very best humans would give you another five times speedup in algorithmic progress.

### More

Throwing more workers at a problem isn’t always helpful. For example, a swarm of agents probably couldn’t write a great novel much faster than a single agent could. But other tasks lend themselves to parallelization: for example, look at the animation below, showing a bunch of different Claudes working together to formalize Fermat’s Last Theorem. This feat had thousands of modular sub-tasks, each of which could be completed independently and where each agent could check locally whether their work had succeeded.

![](/series/how-i-see-the-situation/fig-4.gif)

AI research is closer to formalizing a theorem than to writing a novel, because there are clearer verifiable proxies for progress. That means a swarm of agents can try a thousand different approaches at the same time and see which one performs best.

And the improvements that agents make in an area such as pre-training usually don’t require commensurate improvements in another area, like inference efficiency, to be useful: these improvements often contribute independently. This is why AI companies are already made up of dozens of teams working independently on different parts of the pipeline: one team improves how the pretraining data is filtered, another tweaks the optimizer, another builds a particular kind of coding RL environment, another speeds up the attention kernel, and so on. Myopic hill-climbing agents are particularly well suited to making progress in this kind of situation.

Even so, I expect steeply diminishing returns from just adding more parallel researchers. This is because they may duplicate each other’s work, think in similar ways, step on each other’s toes, and most importantly, split up each other’s compute budget. Compute for experiments is an extremely important input into algorithmic progress. An automated company that adds researchers faster than it can add compute will quickly become bottlenecked by the latter. This wouldn't be absolutely crippling - I explain why in the Appendix - but it’s a huge haircut on how much speedup the company would get from increased parallelization.

But even conservatively, I’d guess that going from the hundreds of researchers of today’s human AI companies to the hundreds of thousands of tomorrow’s automated companies would at least quadruple the rate of algorithmic progress.[^8]

## We might get a singularity, or a spark

So the combination of faster, smarter, and more automated researchers will speed up AI progress a lot. But will this pace continue, will it get even faster, or will it slow down? That question is even more important than what the initial pace actually is.

Two competing trends are relevant here. And the subsequent pace of progress will come down to which ends up being more powerful. On the one hand, because of RSI, automated researchers will continue getting faster, smarter, and more plentiful. On the other hand, AI research will be getting harder and harder, as all the easy ideas get eaten away by the swarm.

By the way, “ideas getting harder to find” is a thing that already happens. AI companies have to quadruple their R&D compute and double their workforce every single year just to maintain the same rate of progress.

If the rate at which AIs get smarter is faster than the rate at which ideas get harder to find, then we’d get something called the “software-only singularity.” In this scenario, even without increasing compute, the AI swarm rapidly makes smarter and smarter versions of itself until, within a matter of months, we end up with a superintelligence that scrapes against the physical limits of our current chips.

But if ideas get harder to find faster than RSI makes AIs better at research, we’ll end up with what I’d call a “spark.” We’d get something like 3 to 6 years of AI progress in a single year.[^9] And then things would simmer down until we build more compute, because even the super-genius automated researchers need to run bigger experiments before they can make further breakthroughs.

If pressed, I’d give a 75% chance to the “spark” scenario and a 25% chance to the “software-only singularity.”

## Even with the spark, we get superintelligence

To be clear though, even the “spark” scenario, where we make ~4 years of progress in the first year after automating AI research, would feel insane. That’s a jump as big as GPT-3.5 to GPT-6 Astra in just 12 months. To remind you of the scale of progress that this leap would compress, GPT 3.5 was able to write a passable limerick and autocomplete a trivial python function, but was mostly a useless toy, whereas Astra can build [whole](https://x.com/ascinhiding/status/2097360365721911448) [video](https://x.com/petergostev/status/2095596341422440714) [games](https://x.com/builtbysketch/status/2098777028078211283) from scratch and churn out Fields-medal-worthy mathematical contributions for breakfast.

In the last four years, we’ve gone from a preschooler to a savant intern. If you made another jump that big, but starting in 2028, you’d probably get a mind which would be vastly superhuman in domains that are even slightly verifiable. For example, it’d be better than Nvidia at coming up with the next generation of AI accelerators. It would make more money than the current human versions of Citadel and Jane Street (if it had equal access to the market). If you gave it [remote access to a material science experiment lab](https://periodic.com/news/building-labs-that-learn), it’d be better than the rest of humanity at finding a room-temperature superconductor.

But they won’t just be good at verifiable domains. That’s because, during RSI, AIs won’t just be training themselves on difficult math and science problems (for example, like rediscovering general relativity using just a pre-1905 data corpus). Rather, automated engineers will build 1000 times more RL training environments than we have currently. And these will be far longer running, higher quality, and more open-ended, and covering a much wider range, than today’s environments.

The AIs will learn to make their way in virtual worlds that are to today’s video games what a Bugatti is to a horse and buggy. These games will be far more difficult, complex and realistic than anything ever made for human entertainment. They will require AIs to cooperate or compete with teams of other AIs over long periods of time. For example, one thing the AIs could be trained to do is to cofound a really successful company inside a simulated digital economy made up of millions of other AIs.

And it’s not just the training data that these automated researchers will be improving. They will also radically improve the basic learning algorithms themselves, through breakthroughs in online learning and sample efficiency (both of which have verifiable proxies, as I discuss in the Appendix).

Even these AIs would need some real-world experience to beat top human experts, since in many fields, the basis of that expertise is years and years of training and context-gathering. However, I think these post-spark AIs will match top human experts within months of deployment.

One thing that’s guiding my intuition here is that I have a lot of 20-something-year-old friends who I’ve watched accomplish incredible things and build world-leading companies and ventures in domains they entered into with basically zero years of experience.

History is full of super-competent young people who ran circles around the world they found themselves in. By the age of 27, Napoleon had stacked victory after victory against far larger armies from the other great powers of Europe. By 25, just two years after he got into the oil business, Rockefeller was running one of the world’s largest refineries.

Rockefeller and Napoleon needed experience, yes, but only a couple years worth, not decades. Whatever was going on inside those guys’ brains, AIs should be able to replicate (or even exceed: there’s no reason to think humans are the ceiling on learning quickly from experience). Furthermore, unlike Napoleon or Rockefeller, AIs can accumulate years of deployed context in just a few months of calendar time. This is because they’ll work at least an order of magnitude faster, and do so around the clock, and they’ll can ingest huge amounts of information very quickly, and will spin up parallel copies to each independently  gain experience before getting merged back together. That’s why I think these post-spark AIs could get to top human performance in almost all domains within months of being developed. That’s 2029 in this modal scenario.

And remember, even in the spark scenario, all of this happens in just the first year or two after AI research is fully automated. Things don’t just stop there. Because even if the software progress peters out, AIs will stoke the fire by continuing to add more compute. Even without help from AIs, [global AI compute has been growing roughly 3x a year](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-chip-production). And the AIs that come out of the spark will be jaggedly superhuman at helping us continue scaling up compute. They’d be extremely good at designing AI chips, laying out the design of a new fab, and even teleoperating robots to help build parts of fabs and datacenters.

So in the spark scenario, AI capabilities continue to improve, but because their progress is driven by building more chips, datacenters, and robots, it takes years to get to the same end point that the software-only singularity would have achieved in months: a superintelligence that is to us what we are to chimps.

In the next essay, I’ll walk through what I expect these minds to be like, and how I expect them to transform the world.

## Appendix to Essay 1

If you’re satisfied with the arguments I’ve made so far, you can stop reading here. But earlier, I promised to explain why I think AI research is largely verifiable – so that’s where I’ll start. After that, I’ll go through the strongest objections to the idea that automating AI research will make progress go much faster, and explain why I don’t find them convincing.

### AI R&D is largely verifiable

There are roughly three broad buckets to AI R&D: pretraining, posttraining, and inference efficiency. As I’ll explain below, each has strong verifiable proxies.

Keep in mind that this verifiability is valuable for two reasons. First, it allows AI companies to RL a model to get better at a specific part of AI research through thousands of rounds of practice. And second, these proxies make it easier for agents to work in parallel, because they can locally check whether they beat a particular leaderboard before merging their contributions.

![](/series/how-i-see-the-situation/fig-7.png)

#### Pretraining

As you’re building GPT-7, you can RL it to get better at finding pretraining compute multipliers. For example, you could make an RL objective that says GPT-7 has to minimize how much compute it uses to train a GPT-2-sized model to a fixed pretraining loss.[^10]

And once GPT-7 is built, you can deploy a swarm of agents to figure out how to train a GPT-3-sized model to the lowest perplexity possible. Loss on a held-out test set, which is the basis of pretraining progress, is extremely easy to verify.

Of course, what works at small scale doesn’t necessarily work at large scale. And it requires taste to anticipate which promising small-scale improvements will actually bear fruit when composed together in a bigger model. But this kind of intuition is also trainable—for example, you can make an RL environment where GPT-7 has to predict whether an algorithmic improvement made at the 100M parameter scale will hold at the 10B scale. And while this “taste-bench” environment is more expensive to RL on than the [nanoGPT speedrun](https://github.com/kellerjordan/modded-nanogpt), it's still not that difficult to train on if you have frontier-lab-scale compute. So even important parts of research taste are verifiable.

By automating pretraining alone, you’d radically speed up AI progress. And there are a couple of important reasons why:

First, pretraining leads to very general improvements in the model’s intelligence and capabilities. There is a deep explanation for why this is true, which has to do with the link between compression and intelligence, and if you want to learn more about this, you should check out [this excellent video by 3Blue1Brown](https://youtu.be/GlYgs6v2YfU?si=z8VqNBJW56aUQwQV).

Second, as you make pretraining improvements, you can train a smaller and smaller model that is just as good as a previously larger model, but which trains faster and more cheaply. Which not only speeds up pretraining but also makes your experiments and RL rollouts atop this base model more efficient.[^11] And since your automated researchers are also instantiations of this now smaller model, you can now support larger researcher populations too.

Third, because smaller models train faster, this tightens the RSI loop itself. During RSI, labs probably won’t do three-month pretraining runs spread across multiple datacenter campuses. The army of automated researchers would be finding algorithmic innovations so quickly that, by the time a giant run finished, it would already be made obsolete by a much smaller model that first began training only a week ago. It’s similar to how, if you launched a spaceship to Alpha Centauri with today’s technology, it would probably be overtaken in transit by one launched a century from now. Furthermore, since you’re training smaller models faster, on a scale where you can run thousands of experiments daily, you won’t have to bet that small-scale results will hold up in bigger models. You’ve already tested at the scale you’re training.

How much better could base models get, even at today’s parameter sizes? I think there’s a ton more potential. We are far from the limits of pretraining. The AI lab Magic recently [announced](https://magic.dev/blog/pretraining) that they pretrained a model that matches the leading open-source base models today, like DeepSeek V4 Pro, for just half of the compute used to train the original GPT-3 (roughly $500k today). Orders-of-magnitude further improvements seem totally plausible

#### Posttraining

Here are three different verifiable proxies for post-training:

First, your automated researchers can try to improve your post-training algorithms such that if you want to RL on a new set of environments, it takes less compute or data or time (all easily measurable).

Second, your automated researchers can work on making models better at learning on the fly, also easy to measure: If you drop the model into an environment it was never trained on, how quickly does it learn what’s going on and figure out how to get a good score? For example, how fast does it get better at a video game it's never played before? You can have more real-world versions of this too. Check out ByteDance’s [EdgeBench](https://edge-bench.org/) for examples of such tasks.[^12]

Third, you can also just measure AI R&D capability directly. Anthropic has an internal benchmark called [CoBench](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/f61d49fa5596956a5dec75fea0e973bf6a6a8378/Redacted%20Risk%20Report%20August%202026%20.pdf), which contains around 500 real problems that their researchers and engineers solved this year. Every benchmark is also an RL environment, and an RL environment like this would allow Anthropic to have their models practice redoing the last three to six months of human progress at the frontier.

![](/series/how-i-see-the-situation/fig-8.png)

#### Inference efficiency

Another important part of AI R&D is designing the model and the surrounding software to get more juice out of your compute. This is especially verifiable. You can easily measure GPU utilization, KV cache size per token, total FLOPs in a forward pass, latency, etc.[^13]

#### Sample efficiency and online learning

One thing to call out explicitly is that, because pretraining and post-training are verifiable, so is progress on what is the most galling and important deficiency that AIs have against humans: namely, their lack of sample efficiency. If you want a neural network to gain some capability, [you have to feed it much more data](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/the-sample-efficiency-black-hole) than it would take a human to learn the same thing.

But every kind of sample efficiency is measurable. You can measure how much data it takes to reach a given pretraining loss, how many tasks it takes to reach a given score in an RL environment, or even how much feedback and time within a single session it takes to reach a desired level of performance just from in-context learning.

Sample efficiency and online learning are really two sides of the same problem: how to learn from real-world data quickly and efficiently. For decades, people have recognized this as the deepest open problem in machine learning – AI’s equivalent of a Millennium Prize Problem.  And mathematics has already shown us what happens to such problems, even when they are very difficult, as long as they are verifiable.

### Objections to RSI

Now I want to deal with the strongest objections to the idea that automating AI research will speed it up a bunch.

I want to be clear that these bottlenecks are serious, and they do tamp down the probability of the most aggressive possible takeoff. This is why I think we’ll probably get 3 to 6 years of progress compressed inside a single calendar year, rather than an overnight singularity.

But I think these bottlenecks won't be especially crippling (and the neck of the bottle is actually pretty wide). Let me explain why.

#### Bottlenecked by experiments?

Earlier, I made this extended analogy between the current acceleration in mathematics and what we should expect to happen in AI research. But there is one big difference between AI research and mathematics. In mathematics, you can simply reason your way, with pen and paper, to new discoveries. In AI research, you need to run experiments to validate your ideas, which take compute and time. And as the number of automated researchers grows, each gets access to a smaller and smaller slice of the total experiment compute pie.

My answer this objection is that each agent will still have access to a ton of compute. Frontier labs have been more than [quadrupling](https://epoch.ai/publications/training-compute-of-frontier-ai-models-grows-by-4-5x-per-year) their compute every single year. If this trend continues, then by the end of 2027, OpenAI could give almost 10,000 agents (the amount involved in solving the Millennium Prize problem) each enough compute to run a GPT-3-sized training run every single day.[^14]

Keep in mind that the final model trained in the original Transformer paper back in 2017 took 3.5 days on 8 P100s – which costs about 50 bucks to rent today (about one-ten-thousandth of the training compute of GPT-3).

If top human AI researchers, like Noam Shazeer and Ilya Sutskever, had enough experiment compute to train a GPT-3-sized model every single day, they might make slower progress than they currently do. But it would be extremely surprising to me if they failed to make meaningful progress altogether, and just sat down and started complaining about how small their cluster was. Now, how productive do you think an AI lab would be if it had 10,000 great researchers, each with this much compute, and each thinking 10 times as fast. You wouldn’t want to write a check to this neolab?

The nature of research and experimentation at the labs today reflects that researcher time is extremely scarce relative to compute – top AI researchers individually get paid hundreds of millions of dollars a year. There’s no strong reason to think labs use their experiment compute anywhere near as efficiently as they could if it was that key bottleneck.

There are two main ways in which the RSI agent swarm will use experiment compute much more effectively than researchers use it today:

- Immediately, they can trade labor for compute. To save valuable researcher time, experiments today are, and should be, very wasteful with compute.  Instead of painstakingly setting up and carefully monitoring lots of small-scale pretraining experiments, a human researcher today might instead kick off a couple of huge multi-agent RL runs in parallel. But if you’re bottlenecked by experiment compute, you can just do more of the former. When those automated researchers *are* doing an RL run, they could read every single rollout transcript and immediately pause and rebuild the whole environment if some task is broken. They could be doing crazy amounts of small-scale ablations of the models to make them oh so slightly better. And when a team of agents decides to combine their compute to do a much bigger run, they could spend many subjective decades designing the most informative pooled experiment, and once it’s done running, they could spend further subjective decades comprehensively analyzing it before designing a new experiment.
- These automated researchers could also come up with a much better science of learning from small scale experiments. Scaling laws themselves are a good example of this – the GPT-4 paper says that they were able to predict that model’s final loss using models trained with 10,000x less compute. One of the things that the smarter and faster automated company would be doing is trying to discover more scaling phenomena like [muP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.03466) (which lets researchers anticipate the optimal hyperparameters for a larger run from 100x smaller models). How many more similar discoveries are out there?  I remember chatting to an AI researcher who thought learning from extremely small neural networks could be incredibly informative. He put it this way: "The gods above must work from the same principles as the ants below.”

#### Bottlenecked by data?

Isn’t data one of the main inputs into AI progress? How will RSI happen without that scaling up at the same rate?

My answer is that data will continue growing during RSI, but the AIs will be the ones compiling it. Producing better training data for both pre-training and post-training is itself an ML research problem, and AIs are getting increasingly better at doing it themselves.[^15]

It is true that data is an extremely important input into AI R&D. Jerry Han and I actually did some [research](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/pretraining-progress-is-mostly-data) into this – we found that for pretraining, over the last six years, more than three times the algorithmic progress has come from data improvements than model improvements.

https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/pretraining-progress-is-mostly-data

However, I don’t think people realize that these “data” improvements in pretraining are very much in line with normal AI research work – basically figuring out which techniques for filtering existing data or generating new synthetic data empirically lead to lower loss on a held out corpus.[^16]

A similar thing seems to be increasingly true for RL. DeepSeek just published a [paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19969) where they talk about how they post-trained their new model. They say that changes to the basic RL algorithm have not mattered nearly as much, and “systematic improvements in the scale, diversity, and verifiability of synthesized data and environments account for essentially all of the observed gains.”

And the way in which those environments are made is becoming more and more automated: “we invest in large-scale, automated pipelines for data synthesis and environment construction … Using difficulty and correctness as reward signals, we iteratively train the model to construct better tasks.”

#### Bottlenecked by finding the right verifiable proxies

Some people might argue that all the verifiable proxies I listed above assume the current paradigm. But figuring out what objective to verify in the first place – choosing which hill to have GPT-7 climb – is itself an important part of AI research. This is what one might call “research taste.” Before 2018, almost nobody thought that minimizing loss on next-token prediction was the most tractable path to AI progress. So nobody would have bothered building the nanoGPT speedrun environments on which the agent swarm could practice reducing the compute needed to get to a certain pretraining loss.

Won’t there be multiple new paradigms that we need to cross in order to get from here to ASI? And won’t each new paradigm have some new associated proxy that we don’t know ex-ante? How will the automated researchers find these during RSI?

I have two responses:

- The transformer was visible on (in fact was invented to beat) the existing translation benchmark – a similar story for reasoning models and pre-existing coding and math benchmarks. Proxies made before a “paradigm leap” seem perfectly capable of capturing large step changes in performance.  This is because AI learning algorithms are quite general.
- It’s not that difficult to come up with paradigm-agnostic outer loop proxies: how many Millennium Prize problems does your model solve; if you train your model on data before 1905, how long does it take to discover general relativity; how fast does it master totally unseen video games; and so on.

All in all, I think the other objections to the impact of RSI are much stronger – I would be very surprised if *this* was the reason RSI ended up not being a big deal.

#### Homogeneity of thought

It might end up being the case that while individual AIs beat individual human researchers, swarms of AIs still lose to teams of humans. This is because different humans think in idiosyncratic, diverse ways, and progress often comes from somebody attacking a problem in a way nobody else had considered. Even if a particular AI is a genius, 10,000 copies might just collapse into an intellectual monoculture that is incapable of finding orthogonal breakthroughs or flaws in each others’ thinking.

I agree that if you just naively copy one mind a million times, you wouldn’t get Florence in the machine. And right now, we’re mostly doing the naive thing when we do multi-agent: taking a model that was trained to work alone, making a bunch of copies, and having them pass messages to each other.

And this isn't working astonishingly well so far.  [Ramez Naam wrote a detailed breakdown of current multi-agent scaling](https://www.rameznaam.com/p/can-a-million-ai-agents-create-superintelligence), where he concludes that more agents mostly buys speed rather than intelligence. [Toby Ord found that if you extrapolate early evidence](https://www.tobyord.com/writing/swarm-scaling) about the returns of scaling GPT 5.6 Sol agents, it suggests that a thousand times as many copies of AIs would get about as much done as a single AI working 50 times as long.

My response to this is that the estimate I provided for how much it would speed up AI progress if there were a thousand times more automated researchers was even lower than this. I conservatively said that there was only going to be a 4x speedup from there being so many parallel AI researchers.

But the more substantive comment I have on these early extrapolations of multi-agent scaling is that they're just not that informative about what multi-agent in a year or two will be like,  because we've barely started training AIs to work together in teams. It's very analogous to the way in which some people got anchored too strongly to early evidence on inference scaling when reasoning models first came out. For example, one year ago, [Toby Ord wrote an article](https://www.tobyord.com/writing/how-well-does-rl-scale) pointing out that models need 10,000 times as many tokens to handle tasks that take a human only 10 times as long. But as Toby himself [recently pointed out](https://www.tobyord.com/writing/ai-scaling-vs-human-scaling), this overhead has shrunk 500x over the year: a task that takes a human 10 times as long now needs only about 20 times as many tokens. And progress hasn't stopped yet. If multi-agent scaling improves at a similar rate, then the AIs of 2028 might be beyond human level at coordination.

Of course, AIs not only need to be good at coordination, but also teams of AIs need to be able to think in diverse ways. How hard will it be to train AIs to think diversely? Well, in order to train AIs to do something with RL, two things need to be true: 1. They need to be capable of doing it in the first place (at least some of the time), and 2. We need a way to measure some proxy of it, so that we can amplify and reward that ability when it is exhibited.

The first is already true. AIs are pretrained on nearly everything humans have published. During RSI, they'll continue improving at predicting that text, and this requires getting good at modeling the different minds that produced it. And those minds are diverse, including all the different scientists who have codified their idiosyncratic ways of attacking a problem through interviews, papers, and books.

But now we need to reward AIs for thinking in diverse ways in a way that ends up being productive to the whole team.[^17] We’ve already discussed how progress toward ML research problems can be verified. To the extent that having diversity of thought helps teams of AIs solve these problems, that should be visible through the verifiable proxies for ML progress. And as a result, it should be possible to reinforce. To the extent it doesn't end up being measurable - that intellectual variety isn’t measurable through greater ML research contributions - then we didn't need it in the first place.[^18]
