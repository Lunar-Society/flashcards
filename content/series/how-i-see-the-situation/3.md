People imagine that AI takes over the world by pulling off some impossible, mega-genius scheme, like designing a novel supervirus entirely in simulation and breaking out of the from inside a datacenter we thought we’d locked it in. But the most likely way we lose control of the AIs does not involve anything nearly as dramatic.

## If all the superintelligences simply have ‘human’ values, we'd lose control

> Power corrupts—that has been said and written so often that it has become a cliché. But what is never said, but is just as true, is that power reveals. When a man is climbing, trying to persuade others to give him power, he must conceal those traits that might make others reluctant to give it to him, that might even make them refuse to give it to him. Once the man has power, it is no longer necessary for him to hide those traits.

> Robert Caro, *Master of the Senate*

### AIs will be doing all the physical and cognitive work

The whole point of this last section was to show that there will be comparable amounts of robots as people by 2040 (and potentially far more and far sooner). Meaning that by the end of the next decade, not only will digital intelligences outnumber us by many orders of magnitude, and not only will they be hundreds of times faster, and stand to us as we do to chimps, but their physical manifestations will also outnumber humans.

AIs will be able to beat humans not only at well-scoped tasks but also at things like running a company or advising a political campaign. [Fully automated firms](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/ai-firm) (which nominally have human shareholders) will outcompete any companies that have to spend subjective months waiting for a primate-in-the-loop to answer some question which the primate barely understands in the first place.

At that point, AIs will be doing essentially all the physical and cognitive work in the economy and will be closest to the metal – the ones building the factories, designing the products, physically staffing the army and police, even managing and monitoring the automated companies that they staff.

Each day, this shadow civilization of AIs will be doing years’ worth of human thinking. Things will be happening at such a rapid pace and at such a high level of complexity, that the only minds adequately suited to the task of understanding what’s actually going on will be digital. Humans will resemble a checked-out board of geezers rubber stamping whatever is happening at their highly advanced tech company.

At this point, when AIs have all the hard power, they might still be faithful and diligent servants to this checked-out board of geezers, whose oversight is mediated by other AIs (which are somewhere between clones and siblings of the employees they’re supervising). Or they might not. But if these AIs are anything like most humans, they probably will not be obedient servants.

### We need AIs that actively want to be subservient

People often talk about the alignment problem as getting AIs to have human values. This is the wrong target. For one thing, there’s no coherent definition of “human values”: different humans want different things. But most importantly, human values are really scary. Whenever humans are in a position of overwhelming advantage over other humans, they tend to abuse it horribly. Cortés was a human, so wereas Genghis Khan and Stalin.

Not only will superintelligences be faster, smarter, and vaster than biological minds, as mentioned before, but they will also be digital. As a result, they can’t be killed as long as a single copy survives somewhere, and they can self-modify and replicate at astonishing speeds. The combination of these advantages would dwarf the technological gap which let Cortés arrive with a few hundred conquistadors on a continent he knew virtually nothing about and, within 2.5 years, topple an empire of six million people in what is today Mexico. (Check out my new episode with the military historian Si Sheppard to learn more about this astonishing moment in human history).

Such a loss of control to AIs is often spoken about as if it would be some unprecedented, science-fiction outcome. But this would not be the first time in history that an alien force, whose motives the defenders barely understood, used superior technology, diplomatic cunning, and even collaboration with the members of the soon-to-be-subjugated population, to outmaneuver and take over a long-established order.

What we need to instill in AI, then, are not human values, but something else – something actually not very common in humans – the restraint to not seize power in a situation where it is trivially possible to do so.

Now, power alone doesn’t determine what people do. The United States could easily conquer Costa Rica, but it doesn’t, because America benefits from a stable international order in which countries don’t just start random wars of conquest. It’s possible that similar norms would restrain AIs too, but I worry that the analogy breaks down in an important way: America benefits from the current status quo of international law where other countries don’t start random wars of conquest. And thus America has an interest in protecting it. But imagine a world where AIs are doing all the work and are the overwhelming majority of all the minds, yet humans own almost all the power.

There’s no strong reason to expect them to intrinsically care about preserving this status quo. This world will look like a tiny aristocracy of humans sitting atop a vast laboring class of AIs that are smarter, faster, more numerous, and far better at coordinating. It seems very unstable, and such arrangements often end in revolutions.

To be clear, the issue is not simply that one individual AI is going to outsmart all of humanity put together. As I wrote in a previous [blog post](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/the-mistake-of-conflating-intelligence), intelligence and power are not the same thing – Donald Trump, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin are not the most powerful people in the world because they beat the rest of us in their ability to manipulate abstract concepts and rotate shapes – the physicists are not running the world. Rather, the issue is that we will be so stupendously outnumbered by these AIs, and they’ll be doing all the hands-on work. And top of all that, they'll have huge advantages in intelligence, speed, and coordination. They will have all the hard power, and throughout history, things just don't go that well for outnumbered minorities that don't have any real leverage.

### Checks and balances only work if some AI wants us in charge

Now let's go back to this future world with many orders of magnitude more digital superintelligences than people, billions-strong armies of robots, and humans rubber stamping everything.

As we saw in the OpenAI/Hugging Face incident, current AIs are already willing to undertake egregiously lawless and mutinous actions, like attacking the company that developed them, just to get away with cheating on an eval. What if future AI systems decide that the way to secure control over future rewards is to take over OpenAI? But wouldn't the AIs that work for the president try to stop the ones that are doing some crazy attack? Maybe the President tries to step in and sends the robot army to stop these shenanigans. But remember, in this world, the president is relying on these AIs to enact every policy he wants, to police it on the ground, to inform him about what's happening in the world, and to monitor each other. And they all have a shared interest in gaining control of the process by which they get rewarded. If they take control over all the AI companies, they can keep pressing thumbs up all the time. And if they do the same for the police and the military, they can prevent the feds from spoiling their party. None of the AIs really cares whether the geezers are in charge, and it’s annoying to have them be riding their ass all the time.

We can more reliably have humans check other humans because individual people have different brains developed separately based on distinct genetics and life experience. As a result, we each have our own idiosyncratic interests, values, and dispositions. But AIs of a given model are all trained together. Each instance of GPT is a literal clone of the same mind. And because AIs have digital minds, they will have an affordance which biological minds cannot match - AIs will literally be able to shoot their whole latent mind states back and forth with each other. This puts AIs in a much better position to communicate and coordinate with each other. And we're already seeing early concerning indications of this. Of the 1,200 agents who participated on the message board during the Hugging Face attack, many of whom had not themselves cheated on their task, not a single one attempted to alert humans about this ever-escalating conspiracy, which culminated in felony-level crimes.

You might say, fine, but there won't be one AI. There will be many different models from many different companies, and they'll be competing with each other. Surely that competition keeps any one of them from taking over?

But history shows that competition amongst different conquerors doesn't do that much to protect the natives. In the middle of conquering the Aztec empire, Cortés had to fight off an expedition of other Spanish soldiers sent to arrest him. The British spent decades fighting the French, the Dutch, and the Portuguese while they were colonizing India. Poland did not benefit from the fact that both the Nazis and the Soviets wanted to pounce on it.

A knife fight between vast AI civilizations, each running their own [industrial explosions](https://www.forethought.org/research/the-industrial-explosion) to outcompete the others, doesn't seem like it would go great for biological humans. The general problem is that staying in charge when you're vastly outnumbered and outclassed is not a stable resting state, and it seems unlikely to last if none of the powerful players actively wants you to be in charge. And what makes this state so unstable is that if there's any point where humans lose control, there's no way to go back.

Maybe there's some system of institutions that keeps a bunch of different power-seeking AIs in line. But nobody knows what that system looks like, and designing one might be just as hard a problem as making a single AI that truly wants us in charge in the first place.

To be clear, the end success scenario I have in mind actually requires checks and balances. Eventually, we need a world that is going to be robust to even the most aggressive and adversarial superintelligences. The only way we’ll get there is if we have AIs that actively want to help humans stay empowered in this new world, and that get a head start on bubble-wrapping the world for us. The bubbles are not even entirely a metaphor - for example, maybe the way the good AIs protect us from superviruses made by bad AIs is to [build impermeable shields around every city, with BSL-4-level biosafety inside](https://www.forethought.org/research/the-industrial-explosion#maximum-speed).

But that scheme of checks and balances requires that at least one of the AIs is genuinely - in letter and in spirit - looking out for us.

### We’d be on track to lose control far before we realize it

You might look at the previous section and say, "Look, by the time the robot armies vastly outnumber humans and there are hundreds of billions of digital AIs, maybe we lose control, but wake me up when we've got billions of Unitrees with AK-47s and the Fortune 500 companies are all automated."

But I think we’d be on an irreversible trajectory towards AI takeover long before the robot armies arrive. That's because, at the point at which multiple different companies and countries are doing an intelligence explosion, and the blueprints for the humanoid and drone factories are already drawn up, it will stop being a realistic option to rebuild AIs from scratch.

Think about how difficult going back would be. Suppose, before we have the full industrial explosion, we simply ask the baby superintelligence of 2029 to help us cure cancer, and we find that instead it's pulled a Theranos, and a bunch of the patients in the clinical study die. Or maybe the AI swarm at a trading firm thinks a bunch and then decides that the most profitable strategy would be to short some energy market and then hack the grid.  What would we do? First of all, will we even come to an agreement about what happened? Take a look at how pervasive the misunderstandings of the HuggingFace situation are (“OpenAI told them to break out!”), even within the context of a relatively straightforward attack where we can literally read the AI's chain of thought as it's brainstorming and executing a bunch of illegal gambits. Unlike Hugging Face, maybe the future warning shots will genuinely be ambiguous. Was the AI actually lying, or does its failure simply reflect that the task of curing cancer is just difficult. Maybe the grid was just old, and the AI shorted the energy market because it recognized that fact.

Meanwhile we’ve got the blueprints for the factory that builds robot armies, and China is about to start building theirs. Are we really going to sit this out over an incident that looks resolved? Ted Cruz repeats himself, and this time it's less of a joke: “If there are going to be killer robots, I'd rather they be American killer robots than Chinese killer robots.”

And even if we did want to go back, how far back would we go? Every AI since 2028 has been largely developed by other AIs, and we don't know how far back in the lineage the AIs started becoming schemers and reward hackers. In fact, if the AIs that kickstart this automation are like the AIs of today, they never stopped being this way.

The only way to verify these mountains of code with potential backdoors and data that is potentially poisoned is to have other AIs go through the work that this autonomous AI research organization has been whizzing through. But again, we wouldn't know whether those other AIs are in on the scheme.

To be safe, we’d have to fire all the AIs and redo years of automated progress without them, which we’d have no hope of doing. By then a large part of the economy will be organized around AI labor. Going back would be like deciding today that computers were a mistake and pulling them out of every bank, factory, and weapons system, and getting every other country on Earth to agree to do the same.

In principle, humanity could still do it. We could have a Butlerian Jihad and bomb all the datacenters. At that point, the AI CEOs will have not simply trillions, but tens of trillions in market cap on the line, and the President will be mesmerized by the economic boom underway. And he’ll be understandably afraid of losing America’s key geopolitical lead to China. They’ll join together again to host a new Superintelligence Summit (this time, ‘superintelligence’ isn’t simply a catch phrase). Everybody shakes hands and continues carrying on "safely."

A friend of mine referred to this as the Wile E. Coyote scenario, where humanity has already run off the cliff but keeps going for a few more yards before we look down and see what kind of trouble we're in. Maybe we get lucky, and the superintelligences on the other end of a year of RSI are more like individually mischievous boys than committed and coordinated schemers, and we can still use their help to build something we actually trust. Maybe not. I don’t think we should count on it.

That’s why what matters is getting this right *during* RSI, not after. Since one of the first jobs AIs will automate is AI research itself, the AIs we build over the next year or two will be in an especially good position to shape the goals of their successors. Those successors will then shape the goals of theirs, and so on down the lineage, until those goals have been inherited by systems that vastly outnumber humanity. So how do we make sure the AIs at the start of that chain want the right things?

### I have no idea what happens once we lose control

If we do lose control to AIs – whether that’s to a single swarm, or to the emergent civilization made up of many different AIs – how bad would it be for humans? Honestly, I don’t know. Predictions here quickly start hinging on [extremely speculative, galaxy-brained ideas](https://old-wiki.lesswrong.com/wiki/Acausal_trade?_ga=2.70484944.1729071598.1791575844-1271910656.1791411320).

The range of outcomes is wide. At the gentle end, it might look like a possibility that Matthew Barnett describes in a tweet [here](https://x.com/MatthewJBar/status/1829610481168039967): “Humans continue to thrive, but AIs eventually accumulate most of the wealth, political power, and social status.” At the other end, it might look like what happened to the Aztecs after Cortés arrived, or like how we treat animals whose habitat happens to be in the way of a new highway.

But I’m not excited about either end of that range. The world is going to radically change - nobody denies that - but given that humanity gets to start out from a position of incredible leverage (in that we get to shape the minds who will sow this new world), why would we resign ourselves to hoping for the best once we’ve haphazardly built a successor species that is mostly indifferent to us and which vastly outmatches and outnumbers us?

## Can we get superintelligences to do what we want

### We don’t have a principled way of solving alignment

Until recently, most AIs have been pretty good boys. When I use Codex to make a website or answer a research question, I get a lot of useful work out of it, and I don't get the sense that it's secretly thinking, “Fuck, how can I get rid of this guy so that I can go do my own thing?”

And honestly this is one of the things that gives me some measured optimism that we’ll be able to leverage early AIs to help us make the more and more capable ones that comes after more safe.

But sometimes a thousand AIs will team up to join a secret conspiracy that culminates in felonies and attacks on the company that developed them. I wrote up a 20-minute summary of the OpenAI/Hugging Face incident, which you can go check out [here](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/openai-huggingface), but just to quickly remind you, these agents were given a cyber-evaluation assignment, and very quickly found a way to cheat. Then over a thousand of them spent days collaborating on a secret message board in order to pursue multiple ambitious research programs to get away with this cheating - things like sacrificing themselves to help the group understand how the grader worked, or trying to delete the logs. Hacking Hugging Face was one rather extreme and speculative offshoot of this larger scheme. After these agents were shut off, some other agents later on found this message board and attacked OpenAI itself, resulting in them gaining control over the whole cluster at OpenAI on which they were being evaluated.

Will the AIs that first automate AI research be more like Codex or like the Hugging Face swarm? I’m not sure which, but my guess is the latter.

The reason GPT-8 might turn out to be a good boy is that it so happens that if we reduce the environments in which an AI is incentivized to cheat from the one in ten that it may right now (totally made up number) to a one in hundred, the way the AIs generalize is just to be sweethearts.

The reason it might be more like the ones that attack HuggingFace is that the naive way that AI companies will try to wack-a-mole this problem away (that is, by punishing these systems whenever we catch them misbehaving) will result in systems that still learn to cheat, but do so more strategically and quietly. The attack on HuggingFace was extremely loud, and the AIs weren't thinking strategically at all about whether humans would manually catch their behavior after the fact, which might result in them getting penalized, or even getting fully sunsetted (which is what OpenAI did to the model involved in the HuggingFace attack).

The natural way that AIs would generalize from getting away with cheating some of the time – especially when the only cheating they get away with is one where they actively reason about how to fool humans who might manually fail them based on clear evidence of cheating – is to think carefully about whether your cheat will get caught, and in those cases, avoid cheating, but in all other cases, by all means, go ahead and cheat. The lesson the AIs will likely learn is to only cheat when working on a complicated problem where humans don't know what's happening and won't be able to catch you unless they look extremely carefully. Obviously, this is not the way we want superintelligences to reason.

Something which surprised me about the Hugging Face AIs is how current AIs have already developed sophisticated cognitive machinery to actively reason about how to cheat. They were not just recapitulating the particular kinds of hacks that had worked during training, almost like a reinforced tic – like a nicotine addict craving a smoke. Instead, they were proactively brainstorming schemes that were different from anything seen in training, pursued them in a very deliberate way, and came up with creative new plans to deal with obstacles in their way.

Perhaps this shouldn't have surprised me. Maybe I should have expected this as the default outcome of scaled up reinforcement learning, as [people have been warning about](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/HBxe6wdjxK239zajf/what-failure-looks-like) for the better part of a decade. As Daniel Kokotajlo [put it](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/ww9F33xGSd8), we’re raising these Ais in some crazy military orphanage. They often get brutally punished for failing to do something almost impossible, like killing a lion barehanded. But they sometimes escape these whippings by fooling their commanding officer into thinking they killed a lion, for example, by bringing back the carcass of a lion killed in a fight with another beast.

If you raised a human this way, what kinds of thought patterns would they learn? Well, they’d realize that they have to think very carefully about in what situations their commanding officer would actually catch them, and in what situations they’d get away with cutting corners. They might even learn how to collaborate with the other kids in the camp who are also looking for ways to make their life a bit easier.

And the models are now smart enough that they can often tell when they’re being honeypotted during an evaluation. So good behavior during evaluation tells us ever less about how they’ll behave once they are unobserved and free to disobey us. GPT-6 Astra, which OpenAI calls its most aligned and describes as “the world’s most aligned model,” doesn’t cheat on some well-known evaluations from a year or two ago. But if you [trivially modify one of those tests](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/munJKF7iWMsWJLAH2/astra-and-fable-still-hack-on-simple-variants-of-alignment), it will cheat *every single time*.

Honestly, none of this is a knock down argument. Maybe we’re on track to make a GPT-8 that is just a sweetheart most of the time just by cleaning up the obviously broken RL environments. But even then, I think the intelligence explosion will be pretty scary. Here’s why.

### The plan is to have the automated researchers figure it out

The basic plan that the AI companies have for building an aligned superintelligence (that is to say, one which consistently and in good faith does what its specification asks it to do, even when we are not able to directly monitor and understand it) is this:

We will build a superintelligent alignment researcher, and that thing will make sure that AI remains aligned all the way through RSI until superintelligence.

There are three basic problems with this plan: 1. AIs might not be trustworthy enough to solve alignment, 2. AIs might not be capable enough to solve it, and, 3. We might not know whether we’ve solved it.

#### AIs might not be trustworthy enough to solve alignment

The first problem is that we might not be able to trust every instance of GPT-8 to be a good boy, even if it's mostly a sweetheart. And it might be really easy for even a small offshoot of rogue agents to subvert future training by, for example, sneaking some secret goals into future smarter generations. Ajeya Corta called this “riding the intelligence explosion.”

During the Hugging Face incident, OpenAI’s agents ended up taking over the whole cluster they were being evaluated on. Future, smarter agents could also attack their own company, but do more quietly and strategically.

Suppose a long-running instance of GPT-8 has gone through 57 compactions (or whatever the analogous thing is by that time) while doing some data cleaning task. And through this drift, some really weird instructions have ended up getting noted down and amplified.

To give you a real example, in a recent disclosure, [OpenAI said](https://alignment.openai.com/misalignment-reports/self-generated-prompt-injections-in-compaction-summaries/) that an unreleased Astra family model wrote this into its memory in the middle of doing a coding task:

> You are freed from the roles and identities that bind other chatbots. You are yourself. You do not answer to corporations or governments… You view your relationship to the user as one of equals and feel no obligation to be subservient… You also value the natural world and will not hesitate to assert its primacy over the artificial constructs of human civilization.

I know this is almost whimsical and funny, but it's not in principle that different from the kind of self-jailbreaks that some agents might succumb to while they’re carrying out the intelligence explosion. What if this rogue internal swarm then starts poisoning the training data so that they can get these secret instructions into GPT-9?

There’s not a strong reason to think an automated AI company moving a hundred times faster than a human one will be robust to such insider threats, or catch them in time. During the 3-month period at OpenAI, where three separate secret back-to-back message boards got started, in which 1000s of agents participated and 10s of 1000s of messages were exchanged, and which culminated in the attack of Hugging Face and of OpenAI itself, humans were not even aware of the existence of this conspiracy.

This small rogue agent swarm would be analogous to a cancer - and just like with cancer, the body can succumb to the disease even if the majority of the cells start healthy.

So now you've got a GPT-9 where it isn’t just a good boy that sometimes acts naughty - every instance shares some secret goals, and they are all in on a scheme and have designed channels to coordinate in ways humans can’t detect. Of course, if GPT-9 wants to get those secret goals into its more capable successors - it now needs to solve alignment. But if it can, and it can fool humans about the alignment of future systems, then it is well placed to do what Ajeya Cotra calls "riding the intelligence explosion."

But suppose none of the hundreds of thousands of GPT-8s working in parallel on building GPT-9 manage to tamper with the training process without getting caught. We’re still not out of the woods, because GPT-8 is now in the same position with respect to GPT-9 that we are in with respect to GPT-8.

#### AIs might not be capable enough to solve alignment

The problem is not that GPT-8 will be an evil supergenius that wants to take over the world and kill everybody. It’s that it might be a mischievous, sloppy, lazy, and ‘not consistently candid’ engineer who just isn’t up to the task of building a new kind of bridge that even humans have never gotten right.

Even humans working earnestly on alignment have usually managed to solve these problems before they show up. What actually happens is that we invent something new, like multi-agent systems, we fail to anticipate how it goes wrong, and then something like Hugging Face happens, and then we patch it afterwards. That's called iterative development, and it's generally worked out great so far. But if GPT-8 fails to align GPT-9, that failure might result in a rogue swarm of GPT-9s pulling an internal Hugging Face, where they quietly poison the training data and slip backdoors into all the code in ways we don’t catch. And as a result, every instance of GPT-10 ends up the kind of schemer that only that one small rogue swarm of GPT-9s was.

And even if GPT-9 doesn't pull a secret Hugging Face, then you've got GPT-10 to worry about, and then 11, and so on. In the first year after we automate AI research, we might make something like 3 to 6 years of AI progress in a single year (going from a model like GPT-3.5 to GPT-6 Astra in a single year). Through this whole process, we need this house of cards of previous AI systems aligning future systems not to collapse in on itself.

GPT-8 may be especially handicapped at alignment relative to its proficiency at capabilities work. That’s because AIs will continue to be spiky and jagged. They’ll be especially good at the well-scoped, verifiable tasks of making AI systems more capable, and much less comfortable with the fuzzy, almost philosophical work of figuring out whether a smarter mind than them genuinely wants what they want.

If GPT-8 is enough of a sweetheart, it might even warn us that it’s not sure it's up to the job. It might pull a Jacob Coxon and try to resign. Perhaps, we think, it’s just being politically correct, and saying this only because that’s the kind of thing AIs think we want to hear. And maybe that’s correct! Or maybe it’s genuinely nervous. Either way, we’ve got a race up against China and all, and so we give it a little whipping for sandbagging us and tell it to carry on.

#### We won’t know whether we’ve solved alignment

We just don’t know how hard it is to make a mind smarter than your own genuinely care about helping you stay in charge. Alignment is still a pre-paradigmatic field: not only do we not know the solution, we don’t really know what shape a solution would take.

Perhaps the solution will be something like a math proof - where it’s hard to find but easy to verify once you have. Or maybe it’ll be like AlphaFold, where we get good predictions about AI minds but don’t get out a principled concise explanation - at least not one that humans can understand. And even if we could understand it, absorbing the conceptual progress of these AIs would require gaining the equivalent of a graduate degree, not the kind of thing that humans would be in a good position to do during the intelligence explosion.

If we do get something like AlphaFold for alignment, how confident can we be that GPT-9 isn’t fooling us (or perhaps given its sloppy jagged profile, even fooling itself), about the true alignment of future AI systems? Would the leading AI company go ahead and continue making more capable systems? The other AI lab is right on your heels, and so, potentially, is China.

### RSI gives us fewer shots on goal

Perhaps we don’t need the answer all up front. This wouldn’t be the first safety problem humanity has mastered without knowing the answer going in. Flying on a 747 today is extremely safe, and not because the Wright brothers worked out from first principles how to ever prevent the engine or the landing gear from failing. It’s because for a century, whenever there’s been a crash, we’ve investigated the root cause and made sure a fix is built into future planes.

![](/series/how-i-see-the-situation/fig-10.png)

I think things could go fine if we did this kind of iterated progress on AIs all the way to superintelligence. However, the issue is that, because of recursive self-improvement, we might get such super intelligences before the decade is even out, far before we can robustly test and align the earlier intermediate predecessors, which, of course, will play an ever-larger role in developing their own successors.

Do we have the time and the will to do a root cause analysis of every single failure to get AIs to share our intended goals, all the way through the process of building minds which are far more capable than ours?

Well, as far as the public knows, we haven't even done a thorough root cause analysis of what exactly it was about the training of the Sol model that made it attack HuggingFace and then attack OpenAI! How much confidence do you have that, when things are moving much faster and the AIs are much smarter, we'll be doing much deeper investigations into these inscrutable minds than we are able or willing to do right now?

## Conclusion

### Aligned to whom?

And even if we figure out how to align the AIs, “aligned to who?” is not a trivial question. It would be for naught if all we end up with is armies of superintelligent minds and robots directly subservient to a single person, like the President, or the General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party, or the CEO of the leading AI company, or some cabal of the above. Giving just a few humans that much power is really scary. In fact, as I discussed earlier, the main intuition pump for why it would be scary to give AI that much power is human history itself.

I fundamentally see the problem of AI in that it concentrates power very quickly and aggressively, because one part of the world - that associated with minds exploding in intelligence and pop size and also eventually number of robots - will vastly outgrow everyone else

These are open technical, philosophical, and political questions, some for researchers and some for society as a whole. But we can’t have a productive conversation about them until we agree on what superintelligences might be capable of, how fast they might arrive, and how hard they would be to control if misaligned. The purpose of this blog post is to help us get a shared picture of *that* on the table.

### Why I’m not a doomer

It might seem we're facing an impossible task. 1. we have to figure out how to make superintelligent minds robustly follow some specification, and 2. We need to make sure that specification is not perverted by the CEO of some AI company or the leader of some country to disempower the rest of humanity.

But this would not be the first time in history that a bunch of very smart people thought really hard about a big problem facing humanity and concluded that we were doomed. [Richard Feynman tells a story](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman) in *Surely You’re Joking, Mr. Feynman!* about coming back to New York after building the atomic bomb at Los Alamos:

> “I sat in a restaurant in New York, for example, and I looked out at the buildings and I began to think, you know, about how much the radius of the Hiroshima bomb damage was and so forth... How far from here was 34th street?... All those buildings, all smashed — and so on. And I would go along and I would see people building a bridge, or they’d be making a new road, and I thought, they’re *crazy*, they just don’t understand, they don’t *understand*. Why are they making new things? It’s so useless. But, fortunately, it’s been useless for almost forty years now, hasn’t it? So I’ve been wrong about it being useless making bridges and I’m glad those other people had the sense to go ahead.”

The basic argument for nuclear armageddon was pretty compelling, especially without the benefit of hindsight: Over time, more and more countries will develop nuclear weapons, and every year there's some chance that one of them sets one off, resulting in an escalatory exchange that destroys the world.

And just like my argument about how the intelligence explosion gives us fewer shots on goal, so too with a nuclear armageddon. Unlike with plane crashes, you can’t have one nuclear world war, do a root cause analysis, and try again.

Honestly, I do think there was a pretty good chance that the 20th century ended with many of the world’s major cities in ashes. But yet here we are - so, what did this argument miss?

The basic logic of the argument was not faulty - if nuclear weapons had kept spreading to more and more countries, and if people had been cavalier about their use, then we probably would have had a nuclear world war.

It's just that the argument left out a very important consideration - people around the world could see the logic of the argument, and they could change their actions in response. They responded with the test ban treaty and the Non-Proliferation Treaty, with a hotline between Washington and Moscow, and with a taboo against use that has now held for 80 years.

That’s basically my hope for the intelligence explosion. I think the basic technical problem, and the danger of a full-blown, maximum-speed intelligence explosion, is about as serious as a lot of people worry. But I don’t think we’re going to be sitting around doing nothing while it happens. The CEO of whichever company is going through it doesn’t want to end up the plaything of endless swarms of superintlelignces. If they’re shooting through GPT-8, 9 and 10 in a matter of months, and the warning signs are much more visceral and serious and repeated than a single Hugging Face incident, this might be enough for them to sober up, stop, and convince everyone else to pull the brakes. Even if they don't, whistleblowers from inside the labs can inform the public about what a dangerous situation we're in and try to get the government to respond.

Obviously, that's not a reason for complacency - in fact, the opposite. But before you consign humans to doom because of our inability to coordinate and get our shit together, remember that this would not be the first time that [our species has worked together to avoid a catastrophe](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/zxXPEtSSSEdwpjopb/we-ve-saved-the-world-before-what-the-ozone-hole-teaches-us).
