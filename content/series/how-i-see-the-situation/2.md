## How superintelligence will supercede us

As I argued in the previous video, just scaling up existing ML techniquesSo, will produce human-level AIs, and the dynamics of RSI will quickly make them super-human at all cognitive domains by [date]. Now I want to talk about what this world looks like. How superintelligent AIs will disrupt the economy, take all jobs, gain dominion over the physical world, and ultimately   making model architectures better, and scaling up reinforcement learning, will probably get us to AIs that are like smart, competent human colleagues. But once we get AIs that are like humans on a server, they won’t stay there forever. In the limit, the capabilities of digital minds can far surpass those of humans.

I’m not yet making any claim about *when* we’ll reach these limits. You might think it will take decades after we have human-level AI. Later, in the section on recursive self-improvement, I’ll argue that automating AI research means we’ll make enormous progress along every one of these dimensions by around 2030, even if we don’t get all the way to the limits. But for now, I just want to articulate along what dimensions superintelligence will outclass us: there will be far more of them, they’ll be much faster, they’ll be smarter and more experienced, and they’ll coordinate in ways we can’t.

### More

An important fact about the future is that the total AI population will grow incredibly quickly. Our intuitions here are naturally very bad because the population of humans cannot grow anywhere near as fast. Because AIs are kind of stupid now, that doesn’t feel like a big deal yet. But as we go from a small country’s worth of human level AIs to many Earths worth of such AIs within a matter of years, this trend is going to hit us like an avalanche.

For at least the last five years, global AI compute supply has been [slightly more than tripling](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-chip-production) per year. Even if that growth slows down considerably, the researcher [Damon Binder estimates](https://defensesindepth.bio/when-they-can-perform-a-task-ais-are-much-cheaper-than-humans/#how-many-agi-workers) that by 2030 there’ll be enough compute to run about 2.5 billion human-level AIs working full time. That’s more than all the white-collar workers in the world.

But it’s not just that physical compute is growing. AI models are also getting better. And one of the consequences of the fact that models are getting better is that small models next year can do what it required big models to do this year. As a result, the inference compute required to achieve a given level of performance decreases over 3x a year.[^1] As long as this software progress trend lasts, you can think of it as the effective population of AIs tripling every year, since that newly freed-up compute can instead run more copies. Keep in mind that this trend might even speed up as we have more AIs working on accelerating this software progress, but we’re getting ahead of ourselves.

If you combine this software progress with even much slower than historic growth in compute, you should expect that the population of AIs at a given level of capabilities will increase more than 5x every single year. As crazy as it sounds to have as many human level AIs are there are people in China and India combined, the year after we hit this milestone, there will be more such AIs then there are people on Earth. And the year after, more than five Earths. And so on.

This exponential growth is very hard to think about. Even if both software progress and compute growth slow down a ton, it’s very hard to avoid the conclusion that a few short years after we have a large country’s worth of human-level AIs, we should expect many tens of billions of such AIs.

### Faster

Not only will there be a lot more AIs, but they’ll also be a lot faster. How much faster? We can start off by looking at how much faster *current* AI completes tasks than humans when we have a head to head comparison. OpenAI made this benchmark called GDPval which compiles over a thousand different tasks across some 44 occupations – things like making a legal brief or an engineering blueprint. And they found that GPT-5.2, which is dumber and more inefficient than the models today, completed these tasks [11 times faster](https://www.inc.com/ben-sherry/openais-latest-model-is-scarily-good-at-these-important-work-functions/91278177) than a human. This also roughly matches how much faster AIs typically output tokens than humans can speak.

We know the ultimate limits here are very high, even if we only keep current hardware. Modern computer chips have a clock cycle that is 1,000,000 times faster than the speed at which our fastest neurons fire. Even if this analogy is [broken](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/adadYCPFAhNqDA5Ye/processor-clock-speeds-are-not-how-fast-ais-think) in a way that makes it 1,000 times off, it seems extremely likely that AIs could think at least thousands of times faster than humans.[^2]

The first superintelligences will not be that fast, but the final superintelligences built through recursive self-improvement could be. If you give that AI a research problem, you can come back a day later, and it’ll have spent many years thinking about it.

![](/series/how-i-see-the-situation/fig-9.gif)

Even today’s models get a subjective year of full-time work out of every calendar week[^3] – it’s just that current AIs are too stupid in most domains to make much use of this advantage. In domains like math, where they are much more competent, you can start to see how big a deal this is. Recently OpenAI reported that 10,000 agents composed of their new internal model solved the Navier-Stokes problem, one of the most important open problems in mathematics. Mathematicians have spent decades trying and failing to solve the Navier-Stokes problem. These agents solved it in four days.

### Smarter

However, simply considering how many AIs there are and how fast they think dramatically understates how big a deal they will be. Every bit of AI progress can be spent in one of two ways: you can run more copies of the same AI, or you can make the AI smarter. And smarter AIs can do things that no number of dumber copies ever could – there’s no realistic quantity of GPT-4 tokens that would have ever solved a Millennium Prize problem.

How smart could a superintelligence be? The ceiling seems far higher than us. As Nick Bostrom puts it in his book *Superintelligence*:

> Far from being the smartest possible biological species, we are probably better thought of as the stupidest possible biological species capable of starting a technological civilization – a niche we filled because we got there first, not because we are in any sense optimally adapted to it.

There are two big reasons to think Bostrom is right.

The first is that even small differences between humans produce massive differences in capabilities. We know that across humans, very small changes in biological design or upbringing can lead to remarkably different competencies. The difference between my brain and Einstein’s is tiny compared to how differently a superintelligence could work.. And yet, I don’t have the genius to unify whole branches of physics. It would be surprising if the relatively minor differences between my brain and Einstein totally exhausted the possible range of intelligence, especially when Einstein’s brain was forced to also use the same puny 20 watt budget, slow serial thinking speed, and general primate brain design.

The second is that human evolution didn’t optimize that strongly for intelligence. We know that there’s a bunch of ways the human brain sucks if you were only optimizing for intelligence. This is because our ancestors faced a ton of other biological constraints on the savannah separate from intelligence. First of all, we’re separated by only a few million years from chimpanzees and bonobos, so evolution hasn’t spent that much time optimizing us for our ability to learn from each other, coordinate in large groups, and think about abstract concepts and plans. But more importantly, even this cherry-on-top optimization was significantly diluted because it had to face off against opposite selection pressure. For example, the brain can’t become so big that it no longer fits through the birth canal. The brain also only gets to consume 20 watts of energy, lest the capabilities that are far more survival-relevant in the savannah than being able to think about abstract ideas (for example, having a well-resourced digestive system) get left out of the budget.

### More experienced

A huge difference between AIs and humans is that every human has to learn their skills and knowledge from scratch in their lifetime. By contrast, in the future, every instance of an AI model will come with millions of subjective years of education in every domain already baked in.

The fundamental reason for this difference is that evolution has no way to package your expertise back into your genes (claims about ancestral memory aside).  Your descendants can't directly inherit all your hard-won lessons in some Lamarckian way. As a result, humanity has had to come up with all these slow, lossy workarounds to share knowledge across generations - like books, schools, and apprenticeship.AI minds don’t have this constraint. They’re digital, so the fully trained mind can be copied again and again, instantly and perfectly. And since each of us gets only about 80 years in this world, it’s impossible for any human to build up expertise as vast and deep as an AI’s, whose training is parallelized and then shared by every copy.

Because we currently don't have ML systems that can learn-on-the-job, the training that comes baked into each AI instance only includes what each model was taught during its roughly 3-month-long training window, as it got pretrained and then RLed on lots of verifiable tasks. But in the future, AIs will also be able to share what they learn from real-world deployment.

Already, at any given moment, hundreds of millions of instances of Claude and GPT are deployed inside all kinds of companies, doing difficult and economically valuable work. And this is the most valuable kind of experience there is. Learning from it would be like getting mentored in the exact job you want to do, rather than majoring in something in college and taking courses that are only loosely related to the work you’ll actually end up doing. AI companies would love to train on this experience if they knew how to. But they haven’t yet solved the technical problem of getting the model to learn efficiently from it. However, getting this continual learning to work is fundamentally an ML problem, and one that will eventually be solved.

Once it is, each instance will learn from experience at least as well as humans do, and that learning will get funneled back into a shared base model. Some instances will be advising politicians, others designing a new computer chip, others building a new high-frequency trading firm, and so on. And the base model would be learning from all of them at once, which means it would accumulate millions of years of real-world experience every single month.  In [my original continual learning blog post](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/timelines-june-2025), I called this possibility “a broadly deployed intelligence explosion.”

### Better coordinated

People [often point out](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/joseph-henrich) that the thing which distinguishes humanity is not our individual intelligence, but rather our capacities to learn from each other, coordinate over vast stretches of space and time, and build up knowledge over many generations. I think this is absolutely right.

To illustrate how important improvements in coordination ability are, compare humans to other primates. Chimpanzees are actually quite smart – for example, they match humans in tests of [working memory](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(07)02088-X?_returnURL=https%3A%2F%2Flinkinghub.elsevier.com%2Fretrieve%2Fpii%2FS096098220702088X%3Fshowall%3Dtrue), [spatial reasoning](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1146282), and competence at [simple mental competitive games](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep05182). The reason why humans can build semiconductors and go to the moon, while chimpanzees can’t, is more the result of the step-change-like consequences in the ability to coordinate.

And coordination is actually one of the key ways that AI will outshine us. I wrote a long [blog post](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/ai-firm) about this a while back. Digital minds can think and communicate thousands of times faster than us. They can instantly spin up thousands of exact clones of themselves, who know everything that they know and go on to solve different sub-problems. And because they can share their direct mind states with each other instead of having to titrate their thoughts over spoken language, a team of a million AIs could work on a single problem with the coherence of one mind.

### A little intelligence goes a long way

When you put all of this together, you have billions of minds, each faster and smarter than any human, each with millions of years of experience, and all working together more seamlessly than any human team ever could. Many people think that even a world like that won’t look that different or feel radically transformed. The reason they give is that we currently have millions of decently smart AIs running around, yet things feel roughly the same. But I think this is the totally wrong takeaway. The reason the world feels the same is that today’s AIs are not only not superintelligent, they’re well below human level in many important ways, and this craters their overall usefulness. Again, even if you had a million derpy amnesiac interns (which is what current AIs are in most domains outside of software engineering), your work wouldn't be that accelerated. If anything, having to manage them all and double-check their work would probably slow you down.

But once AIs near and surpass human-level capabilities, their usefulness will skyrocket. Just look at coding models. The horizon length of the tasks they can do has been increasing steadily, but the value these models generate for programmers has skyrocketed as they’ve gotten closer to human level. As AI systems near and then surpass human-level ability at every cognitive domain, we should expect a similar explosion in their usefulness at each one.

And what happens once AIs get far past human level? Here, I think, it’s genuinely impossible to anticipate. If an alien were watching our ape ancestors in the forest six million years ago, I think it would have a really hard time reasoning from first principles about what their descendants could do with brains three times larger. The alien would guess that we’d become smarter. But would it guess evolution would repurpose our ancestors’ vocal tracts and nimble fingers to transmit immensely complicated, abstract thoughts across time and space? Even language, something which seems so inevitable, would be hard to predict in advance.

And the gap between chimps and us is tiny on the scale of possible minds. Today’s AIs are able to solve a Millennium Prize problem. AIs five years ago could barely write a coherent sentence and had no real capacity to even engage in a conversation, much less do any useful tasks. All AIs ten years ago could do was label images. And fifteen years ago, deep learning was a backwater field which had basically produced no useful applications. It’s likely that 10 more years of progress at this pace would cross a gap bigger than the one evolution stumbled through over the last 6 million years since we split off from the chimpanzees.

## AGI will lead to an industrial explosion

At this point, a lot of people mightwill say, "Well, okay, you've got a planet's worth of superintelligences in your datacenters. But so what? What actual real-world impact do they have?”

It’s time to So now let’s talk about robots. And bear with me, because pulling on this thread this will lead us to the industrial explosion and the technological explosion.

### Robots are bottlenecked by intelligence

#### Robotics hardware is mostly good enough

In robotics, software, not hardware, is the bottleneck. Our robot models suck:, but when we have humans teleoperate, robots, they can be made to do useful physical work. For example, in situations where you can't have a human present (like [constructing things undersea](https://www.oceaneering.com/rov-services/rov-systems/), or doing [extremely precise surgery](https://youtu.be/QUY0Jl-di8k?si=FAOK_4sOtRHDTH7F)), human teleoperated robots are able to get the job done.

Of course, we almost never deploy robots outside of situations where they’re absolutely necessary. Because if you still need to hire a human to teleoperate them anyways, now you’ve paid for both a human and a robot.

Now, there are some ways in which mechanical bodies are currently inferior to human bodies - our hands are very dexterous and get tactile feedback, our joints are more springy, and so on. Even when teleoperated, today’s robots would struggle to do ballet or produce calligraphy.

But the robot bodies today are actually pretty good, and they’re improving rapidly. I think you'd be surprised by how good the demos today are.  For example, here is the new hand that Boston Dynamics has designed.

And here is a Boston Dynamics robot trying to play soccer.

Some people might argue that these are fake marketing demos, where the robots have been trained on lots of human data in the exact kind of scenario shown in the video. But that's exactly my point. If the intelligence of these robots was as good as the humans teleoperating them, then we'd see this kind of behavior not just in the tasks which these companies made human demonstrations for, but rather for all their behavior.

You might object that these are marketing demos – the robots were trained on piles of human data for exactly the tasks they’re showing. Or maybe there's even a human teleoperating them behind the scenes. But that’s my point: when given the right human imitation data or teleoperation, the robot body is clearly capable of doing a bunch of different complex physical tasks.  What's missing is the brain.

### AGI will be able to teleoperate robots

TAnd the brain will get solved as a byproduct of building AGI. There’s something about the human brain which allows you to learn to teleoperate a robot with just a few hours of practice, and to do so far better than any current AI model can. It’s the same faculty that lets you master a new video game quickly. Whatever that faculty is, it's going to get hill-climbed to hell during RSI.

It’s already improving between different model generations: the newest frontier models, which were not specifically trained for robotics (but have decent spatial reasoning and overall intelligence as a byproduct of their normal training) and are much better than even dedicated robot models at teleoperating different kinds of robotics hardware.

There’s one example where Astra gets access to a robot arm it has never controlled before, figures out how to use it, and then paints a picture of the Golden Gate Bridge.

There’s another where someone asks Astra to take control of a Toyota Corolla’s steering, gas, and brakes, and it manages to successfully steer it around a parking lot.  Again, OpenAI was not trying to train a self-driving AI here.

#### Superhuman researchers will be especially good at making robotics better

And even if human-like teleoperation doesn't come along for free with greater intelligence, it certainly will as a result of millions of superhuman automated software engineers and ML researchers that will be trying to make AIs better at robotics.

Just think about what would happen if you handed these automated researchers a warehouse full of 10,000 humanoids. At one station, robots are learning to weld sections of a ship’s hull. At another, three of them work together to slide a GPU server into a rack and plug in its power and network cables. At a third, they’re taking apart a jet engine and rebuilding it to spec. If each robot generates a dozen practice tasks an hour, the warehouse produces over 100,000 training sample an hour.

Within that same hour, the AIs can do tens of thousands of subjective years of engineering. They can train robot models in simulation, and rewrite the simulation engine from scratch whenever a real attempt disagrees with its predictions. They can brainstorm 100,000 gripper designs for a task that keeps failing, test them all in simulation, and 3D-print the winner.  And after studying every frame of footage, they can redesign the task itself, reordering the steps to suit robots instead of humans.

They can even train these AIs to use robots in ways no human can. No human can control eight arms as naturally as Doctor Octopus, or coordinate a hundred different robots to lift a steel beam as if they were a single body, or watch a thousand separate camera feeds at once to guide their movements.

### The path to 20 million robots by 2030

So once we have robots that can do most physical work, how long will it be before we drown in robots? Let’s consider both the demand and the supply side.

#### Demand side

When I talk to people about this crazy robotics stuff, one of the first objections they have is: "Who will buy all these robots? Where will the demand for them come from?” The demand is already here. Around the world, workers are paid tens of trillions of dollars a year in wages to do physical labor.

And sometimes people say, "Look, but we won't want to hire robot nannies.” But even if you excluded *all* services, you’d still end up with a multi-deca-trillion dollar TAM for robot labor. That’s because a large fraction of the economy is composed of sectors like construction, transportation, factory production, and agriculture, where employers just care about the end product of labor.

Employers will have every incentive to replace humans with robots, because robots will be able to do these jobs much more cheaply. Suppose that by 2030, a well-manufactured, versatile, and dexterous humanoid robot costs $30,000 (for reference, the [cheapest Unitree humanoids today cost $5,000](https://www.thepricer.org/how-much-does-an-unitree-humanoid-robot-cost/)). If it can do physical labor which pays $20 an hour, and it can work around the clock, then it can earn its own value in 2 months. This is why I expect enough demand to justify building at least billions of robots.[^4]

#### Supply side

How quickly could we build enough robots to meet this demand? My guess is that, by 2030, the world would be producing at least 20 million humanoid robots per year.

For reference, the global car industry makes about 100 million vehicles per year, and as [Elon often points out](https://www.robotics247.com/article/tesla_shares_more_details_about_optimus_humanoid_robot), robots and cars are made of the same stuff: batteries, motors, sensors, and chips. If anything, producing humanoids at one-fifth the rate at which we currently produce cars feels conservative, since humanoids are over an order of magnitude lighter.

Maybe you agree that robot factories could *theoretically* produce 20 million robots per year, but you aren’t convinced that we can build them by 2030. But just look at Tesla’s Shanghai Gigafactory: they broke ground at the beginning of 2019, and by 2020, just this one plant could produce over 200,000 cars a year. And building it only cost $2 billion. For reference, Big Tech is on track to spend over $700 billion on AI datacenters this year alone.

### Physical production will double once a year after full automation

The most important thing to keep in mind here is that, unlike with cars, robots can help you build more robot factories. As soon as a robot comes off of the factory line, you can send it to the next construction site and have it start welding beams and operating cranes. So you end up with this exponential process where 10 million robots become 20, then 40, then 80, and so on.

But of course, you need much more than labor to make more robots. As you expand production, you need to mine more ore, then you need it to refine it into steel and copper. You need to transport those materials from wherever they’re processed to the final assembly plant. You even need to expand the production of electricity. There are thousands of such inputs, which each themselves have a thousand inputs.

There's this classic [essay](https://cdn.mises.org/I%20Pencil.pdf) from 1958 about how no one person knows how to make a pencil. Just to make an object this simple, you need loggers, miners, chemists, ship crews, and millions of other people to contribute their own small piece of the puzzle. And robots are a lot more complicated than pencils. So the pace at which we produce more robots might be bottlenecked on the rate of growth of the entire economy.

Right now the economy grows about 2% a year. The reason it’s not faster is that we reinvest very little of what the economy produces into building more productive capacity. Four-fifths of everything the economy produces is consumed, and most of the remaining fifth goes to repairing broken machines and worn-out buildings. That’s because, right now, there'd be no point in doubling the number of factories you have every year. Human labor won't double every year, and without those workers, your factories will just sit empty.

But what happens when the labor, which will just be robots, can grow as fast as the factories? The researcher [Damon Binder investigated exactly this question](https://ai-frontiers.org/articles/agi-will-set-off-an-industrial-explosion). He started with this [idea that John von Neumann came up with in 1937](https://ai-frontiers.org/articles/agi-will-set-off-an-industrial-explosion) - think of the economy as a big web of production processes, where every industry’s output becomes some other industry’s input. So if each industry just made outputs for the next one at maximum tilt, without taking anything out for consumption, how fast could this whole loop grow? Damon answered this question by using the data that the government has painstakingly compiled about what every industry buys from every other, and he found that the whole economy[^5] could double every year.

There are a ton of objections you might have to this simple model. What about [energy](https://defensesindepth.bio/the-ai-industrial-explosion-part-4-cheap-power/)? What about [raw materials](https://defensesindepth.bio/ai-industrial-takeoff-part-1-maximum-growth-rates-with-current-technology/#appendix-c-resource-extraction)? What about [construction delays](https://defensesindepth.bio/the-ai-industrial-explosion-part-2-transition-dynamics/) and [supply chain bottlenecks](https://defensesindepth.bio/the-ai-industrial-explosion-part-6-choke-points-to-growth/)? Damon has investigated all of these in detail (which I’ve linked to). He finds that these wouldn't substantially slow the growth rate until the economy becomes 1,000 times bigger than it currently is. By that point, economic stagnation will be the least of our concerns.

This industrial explosion will be driven by this unprecedented opportunity to make the highest returning investments of all time. If a robot pays for itself within just a couple months, then producing it returns hundreds of percent a year. And if the production of such robots is bottlenecked by other upstream inputs, then the returns to investing in those inputs will also be enormously high.

### Diffusion/politics/baristas won’t stop the industrial explosion

I just ran through a pretty wild scenario for the next decade. You might have a bunch of objections - won't this explosion be slowed by diffusion, or regulation, or the fact that at some point, people won’t just keep buying a bigger and bigger library of robots.

I'll deal with each of these points in a second, but first let me address what I’ve found is often the real objection lurking beneath these stated ones: many people are actually just skeptical that AIs will be able to do everything humans can do. So they quibble about diffusion or demand because they’re imagining a future full of derpy AIs and clunky robots. But that’s not what I’m imagining. In my last essay, I laid out why I think we'll have AIs that can match the top human experts in all fields by the end of 2030. And earlier in this essay, I explained why soon thereafter, I expect robots to be able to do almost everything humans can do in the physical world too.

It's totally fine if you don't agree with my timelines. Timing doesn’t change the basic argument that, whenever we do get human-level AIs and robots, we should expect a crazy industrial explosion. So even if AGI arrives in 2040, I think the economy will still double once a year, just starting in the 2040s.

Or maybe you think we'll just *never* get to human-level AIs and robots. If that’s the case, I think we just have a metaphysical disagreement about whether there's some magic pixie dust that enables humans to do what we do. But we're not going to settle that in this essay.

#### Diffusion

Okay, let’s start with diffusion. The objection here is that, with every previous technological shift, there has been a long lag between when a technology was invented and when it actually increased productivity.

Take electricity. Edison’s light bulb came out in 1879. Yet by 1899, a full two decades later, electric motors still provided less than 5% of the mechanical power in American factories. It wasn’t until almost 1920 that electricity became the main source of factory power and started meaningfully improving manufacturing productivity.

Why did it take 40 years? One reason is that factories had to be redesigned from scratch to accommodate electric motors – you couldn’t just plug them into existing systems. We won’t experience this type of lag with AGI. If humanoid robots are actually human-level, then they can step straight into whatever slots companies already have for the humans who would have otherwise been doing the work.

This point is worth reiterating. Unlike previous automation - unlike even current LLMs, which are brittle and derpy and need a lot of handholding - AGI will be like hiring a person. And companies hire people all the time. Across the world, they hire hundreds of millions of new employees a year. After a bit of onboarding, experienced professionals who join a new organization know how to diffuse themselves.

And even if companies refuse to make the switch on principle, it won’t meaningfully slow down the industrial explosion. Because I’m not imagining JPMorgan and IBM and your local hospital automating themselves as fast as they can. I’m picturing something closer to fully automated factories in the desert, growing exponentially and swamping existing firms.

What I'm describing here is continuous with the way the economy already transforms itself, just on a more compressed time scale. Of the companies that were listed on the Fortune 500 in 1955, [only about a tenth](https://fee.org/articles/comparing-1955s-fortune-500-to-2019s-fortune-500/) of them are still on the list. The rest were replaced by faster-growing competitors and companies in entirely new industries. During the industrial explosion, this churn will happen in years rather than decades.

If the fraction of the economy that fully automates starts out at just 1%, but doubles every single year (while the rest of the economy continues with 2% growth), then within 7 years, it will make up over half the economy.[^6]

At best, holdouts can delay the onset of the industrial explosion by a couple of years, not stop it, nor make it so that it’s any slower once it reaches crescendo.

#### Demand

It seems obvious why there will be insane demand for robots, at least at first: they’ll be able to do the billions of different jobs that humans today are already hired for, and do them all more cheaply. But will that demand be strong enough to sustain doubling after doubling of world production? I really want to emphasize that this question only becomes relevant once you have billions of robots that have already automated most of the jobs that humans today are doing. And that alone would already be a radically different economy than the one we have today.

But some people argue that things might just stop there, because by the time you’ve already got a cheap self-driving car and robot butler and so on, people will be satiated on the kinds of things robots can produce, and instead what they’ll want to spend the rest of their money on is [coffee made by human baristas and performances delivered by human ballerinas]( https://aleximas.substack.com/p/what-will-be-scarce), and the supply of those doesn’t grow at the pace of the industrial explosion.

To be clear, I think it’s likely that over time the price of a ballet ticket rises relative to the price of a robot butler. My argument isn’t that the value of each marginal robot stays the same. I’m just saying it won’t crash to literally zero. At some price, people will want 10 times the physical goods and remotely deliverable services they have now. We can see this from the fact that a 99th-percentile American consumes about 10 times as much of that stuff as the average person in the world. And even the 99th-percentile American isn’t fully satiated on the nice things cheap physical production can buy.

But at the end of the day, the industrial explosion doesn’t even need consumer demand. It only needs at least one person, like Elon, who wants his industrial base to keep expanding very rapidly. That's because robots are productive assets, and they can make more of themselves even without consumers asking for it. The rest of us might be satisfied with a couple of robot butlers and spend our remaining wealth going to the opera. But in that world, most of the physical production in the world will just be controlled by and dedicated to the people (or AI systems) who are not easily satiated on more robots.

### Regulation

Of course, all this assumes Elon is allowed to build robot factories that build robot factories. People already hate AI. How much more will they hate it when there are cities in the desert churning out literal robots that are coming to take their jobs? In that world, won’t voters just outlaw the industrial explosion?

My answer is basically the same one I gave to the objection that JPMorgan and IBM won’t adopt AI as aggressively as they could.  It just takes one country that is not willing to actively ban all forms of automation and AI for the industrial explosion to happen. If that country starts with 1% of world output today, it'll be most of the world economy within a decade.

And once everyone sees this geopolitical and military game theory -  that a country which starts the industrial explosion a year earlier will have twice as many robots, drones, and everything else physical -  most of them will probably also rush to have the industrial explosion themselves. As [Ted Cruz put it recently](https://www.forbes.com/sites/saradorn/2026/09/09/ted-cruz-says-he-prefers-american-killer-robots-over-chinese-after-ai-extinction-warning/), “If there are gonna be killer robots, I’d rather they be American killer robots than Chinese killer robots.”

## AGI will lead to a technological explosion

An important thing to keep in mind is that Damon's calculation, that the economy could double once a year, is a very conservative floor. Because it's setting a lower bound, assuming no further technological progress. It's saying, given today's existing production recipes for steel mills and power plants and solar panels, if you just hand the work to robots, how fast could that setup copy itself?

But maybe, just maybe, the billions of superintelligences, who have an industrial base that is doubling every single year, might make more scientific and technological progress.

Now, it’s not obvious that even superintelligence can just think its way to new technology. Maybe it can. But throughout human history, scientific and technical progress has required tons of real-world inputs that aren’t just reasoning. You need experiments, and experiments often require building totally new tools, which are often themselves bottlenecked on other branches of the tech tree. For example, we couldn’t work out the structure of DNA until X-ray crystallography let us photograph molecules.

On top of that, new ideas keep getting harder to find. A hundred years ago, the Wright brothers could build the first airplane in their bicycle shop. Today, for Boeing to build a new passenger jet takes tens of billions of dollars and thousands of engineers. Economists have measured [this same pattern in industry after industry](https://web.stanford.edu/~chadj/IdeaPF.pdf).

And a large part of this progress is not even research lab experiments, but rather just [learning by doing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experience_curve_effect) - the reason that solar panels today are hundreds of times cheaper than they were 50 years ago is largely because we've built a lot more of them, and every time a new factory gets set up, it tweaks the process a little bit to get slightly higher performance for slightly cheaper.

So if human history is any guide, even superintelligence will need real-world inputs to keep scaling in order to radically speed up technological progress. That's why I think it's unlikely they just invent crazy nanotechnology on day one.

But during an industrial explosion, all of these real-world inputs are growing incredibly fast too.  That includes the research labs, the tools, even the rate at which new factories are getting put up (and thus the rate at which you’re making process tweaks and learning by doing.

And the learning we're doing is also sped up by the fact that the AIs are also doing a lot more doing. The robots are working around the clock, and the superintelligences directing them are thinking thousands of times faster, and there's vastly more of them. Again, the crucial thing is that almost every plausible input into scientific and technical progress, not just thinking, is also growing during the industrial explosion. The only thing that doesn't speed up is whatever literally depends on calendar time passing.

Some things are actually bottlenecked by literal time. If you want to understand how Alzheimer's works, you might just have to watch it progress in real patients over the course of a decade.  It's unfortunate that these serial time bottlenecks seem to fall disproportionately on the study of human health and disease. If you look at the sectors which are much more part of the bread-and-butter singularity (energy, manufacturing, computing, space), there's no process which is rate limited principally on the progression of years of calendar time. So it’s quite possible that we end up with mosquito-sized drones and millions of tons of cargo going to orbit before we've cured Alzheimer's.

How much faster could technological progress get? The standard model that economists have for technology growth suggests that if researcher inputs doubled every year (rather than growing at the 4% a year they do today), then technology would advance 16 times faster than it does today.[^7] But, of course, that technological progress would then speed up the growth rate (again, doubling a year is based on today’s technology).  Economists' model of what happens next [produces a technological singularity](https://web.stanford.edu/~chadj/AI.pdf).

Footnote: Of course, long before that, we’d hit the physical limits on how fast anything can grow, which probably comes down to how fast we can produce energy. Damon Binder has calculated that a stripped-down setup of ultra-thin solar film and cheap batteries could repay the energy it took to build within days, so energy production might double every few weeks. Other bottlenecks might stretch that to months. But at this point, we’re just haggling over exactly how astonishingly and unbelievably wild the singularity will be.

But suppose that we don't get the technological singularity. Instead, we have this speedup of tech progress where, conservatively, we get a century of progress in less than a decade. How wild would that be? In their essay, [“Preparing for the Intelligence Explosion”](https://www.forethought.org/research/preparing-for-the-intelligence-explosion), Will MacAskill and Fin Moorhouse lay out an intuition by asking us to imagine what it would feel like if the last century of technological progress got squeezed between 1925 to 1935.

> The first nonstop flight across the Pacific would take place in late 1925. The first footprints on the moon would follow less than four years later, in mid-1929. Around 200 days would have separated the discovery of nuclear fission (mid-1926) and the first test of an atomic bomb (early 1927); and the number of transistors on a computer chip would have multiplied one-million-fold in four years.

In 1955, John von Neumann wrote this essay called “Can We Survive Technology?” It's brilliant, and I highly recommend reading the whole thing. Many of the things he's thinking through are directly relevant to the world today, even though, of course, he’s not specifically talking about AGI.

One of the points he makes is that if technology keeps speeding up, then it will soon be going far faster than humans can react and our institutions can adapt. World War II lasted 6 years. If you get a century of technological progress in a decade, that kind of war would have the armies start with biplanes and horse cavalry and end it with nuclear ICBMs and fighter jets. Even if that happens during peacetime, there's still a crazy escalation in the nature of military power over just six years. How will human political leaders cope with these kinds of changes? How will our human institutions figure out how to manage and steer these developments for the better?

And will human leaders and institutions even be the ones that make the call? We've got this self-replicating autonomous industrial machine that's doubling every year, and which is also making technological progress 10s  of times faster. Then, within a few years, it will dwarf everything else on Earth that hasn’t also gone through the industrial and technological explosion. Which humans get to decide what happens in this autonomous zone? Will any human realistically get to decide? That's a question I'll take up in my next essay.
